## 1. Csikszentmihalyi's systems model

### The core texts

- Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1988). "Society, culture, and person: A systems view of creativity." In R. J. Sternberg (Ed.), *The Nature of Creativity: Contemporary Psychological Perspectives* (pp. 325–339). Cambridge University Press. `[V]` — reprinted as Chapter 4 in *The Systems Model of Creativity: The Collected Works of Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi* (Springer, 2014), [DOI 10.1007/978-94-017-9085-7_4](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-94-017-9085-7_4); [APA PsycNet record 1988-98009-013](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1988-98009-013).
- Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1996). *Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention*. HarperCollins. `[T]`
- Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1999). "Implications of a systems perspective for the study of creativity." In R. J. Sternberg (Ed.), *Handbook of Creativity* (pp. 313–335). Cambridge University Press. `[T]` (pagination sometimes given as 313–338)

### The move that defines the model

The 1988 chapter's opening gambit is the whole argument in one line: countless works have asked *What is creativity?*, but no one asked the question that must precede any attempt at defining, measuring, or enhancing it — **"Where is creativity?"** `[V]`

The answer: not in the person. Creativity is located in the interaction of three subsystems, mapped exactly onto the chapter's title:

| Title term | Subsystem | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Culture** | **Domain** | a symbolic system — rules, notations, techniques, prior works, values `[V]` |
| **Society** | **Field** | the gatekeepers: the persons who control access to the domain and decide what enters it `[V]` |
| **Person** | **Individual** | the site where a variation on the domain's information is produced `[V]` |

Often abbreviated **DIFI** or **DPFi** (Domain–Individual–Field interaction). `[V]`

The operative claim, in the form the literature uses it: an individual produces a **variation** in the information of a domain; the **field selects** which variations are worth preserving; the domain **transmits** the selected variation to the next generation. Creativity is the name we give to a variation that survives field selection and enters the domain. It is therefore an **attribution**, not a property. Formulations across the 1996 and 1999 texts that I can state with confidence in substance (exact page numbers `[UNANSWERED]` — I could not fetch the source text this session):

- Creativity is any act, idea, or product that changes an existing domain, or transforms an existing domain into a new one. (1999)
- Creativity does not happen inside people's heads, but in the interaction between a person's thoughts and a sociocultural context.
- Creativity can be observed only in the interrelations of a system made up of the three parts.

> **Do not quote these as verbatim with page cites without checking the originals.** The substance is standard and uncontested in the secondary literature; the exact wording and pagination is what I could not confirm.

The evolutionary framing (variation → selection → transmission) is the model's actual engine and it is what makes the field non-optional: without a selecting field there is no selection, and with a different field there is a different selection. Note also Csikszentmihalyi's explicit temporal corollary — later generations revise earlier assessments, especially at the paradigm-shifting level. Kaufman et al. cite exactly this point (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996) when noting that expert panels' combined evaluations are "assumed to be the best possible assessment of creativity in that domain **at that time**." `[V]` — Kaufman, Baer, Cole & Sexton (2008), *CRJ* 20(2), p. 171–172.

### The measurement implication, stated explicitly

The claim you asked me to source: *if creativity is a field judgment, then any creativity score is a function of the judge population, and the judge population is part of the construct, not measurement error.*

Nobody I found phrases it in exactly those words. But the two halves are both stated explicitly in the literature, and they compose:

**(a) The construct is defined by the observers.** Amabile's operational definition is the load-bearing one. Amabile, T. M. (1982). "Social psychology of creativity: A consensual assessment technique." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 43*(5), 997–1013. At p. 1001: a product or response is creative **to the extent that appropriate observers independently agree it is creative**; appropriate observers are those **familiar with the domain** in which the product was created; creativity is therefore the quality of products judged creative by appropriate observers. `[V]`

This is an *explicitly operational* definition — Amabile says so. There is no criterion behind the judges. Judge agreement is not evidence about creativity; it constitutes creativity. That is the CAT's whole epistemology and it is why the CAT is the natural measurement arm of the systems model: CAT judges are a scale-model field.

**(b) Changing the judge population changes the answer, and this is not error — it is a different question.** Kaufman, Baer, Cole & Sexton (2008), pp. 175–176, verbatim from the primary text `[V]`:

> "The validity of the CAT is grounded in the fact that experts in a domain are the final arbiters of what is creative (or otherwise valued) in a domain."

and, on the divergence between expert and novice ratings:

> "Are such novice judgments simply invalid? … As direct substitutes for the ratings of experts, they clearly lack replicability … But that does not make the ratings of the novices meaningless."

They then work the People's Choice / MTV Movie Awards vs. Academy Awards contrast and conclude: **"It depends on the goal of the evaluation."** If you want to know what moviegoers enjoy, the novice panel is the *better* instrument; a studio forecasting box office should prefer novices over critics. `[V]`

That is the claim in substance: the two panels are not a good and a bad measurement of one construct; they are measurements of two constructs, distinguished by which field is doing the attributing.

The same paper is equally blunt that reliability does not rescue you `[V]`, p. 175:

> "a high coefficient alpha only shows that the experts tended to agree… A high interrater reliability shows that there was something to rate… The validity of the ratings, however, cannot be assured merely by high interrater reliability alone… Expert judges may still use inappropriate standards, not understand the rating instructions, or have a biased agenda."

**This is the single most important sentence in this entire report for your use case.** High agreement among a panel proves the panel shares a metric. It says nothing about whether that metric is the domain's.

## 2. Sternberg & Lubart's Investment Theory `[T]`

- Sternberg, R. J., & Lubart, T. I. (1991). "An investment theory of creativity and its development." *Human Development, 34*(1), 1–31.
- Sternberg, R. J., & Lubart, T. I. (1991). "Creating creative minds." *Phi Delta Kappan, 72*, 608–614.
- Sternberg, R. J., & Lubart, T. I. (1995). *Defying the Crowd: Cultivating Creativity in a Culture of Conformity*. Free Press.
- Lubart, T. I., & Sternberg, R. J. (1995). "An investment approach to creativity: Theory and data." In S. M. Smith, T. B. Ward, & R. A. Finke (Eds.), *The Creative Cognition Approach* (pp. 269–302). MIT Press.

**The metaphor.** Creative people "buy low and sell high" in the world of ideas: they pursue ideas that are unknown or out of favor but have growth potential, develop them, and move on once the field has caught up and the idea has become conventional. The resistance the crowd offers is a feature of the model, not friction — hence *Defying the Crowd*.

**The six resources** (confluence is multiplicative-ish, not additive: deficits in one can be partly compensated, but below-threshold levels in some resources cannot be bought off):

1. **Intellectual abilities** — specifically the synthetic (redefine problems, see them anew), analytic (recognize which of one's own ideas are worth pursuing), and practical-contextual (sell the idea to others) abilities from triarchic theory.
2. **Knowledge** — enough to move the field forward; but the model explicitly notes knowledge can entrench, so it is a double edge.
3. **Thinking styles** — a *legislative* style (preferring to create one's own rules and structures) from Sternberg's theory of mental self-government.
4. **Personality** — tolerance of ambiguity, willingness to take sensible risks, willingness to surmount obstacles, self-efficacy.
5. **Motivation** — task-focused intrinsic motivation.
6. **Environment** — a context that supports and rewards creative ideas.

**Is it measurable? What did their own tests show?** Lubart & Sternberg (1995) ran studies in which adults produced creative products across domains (writing, art, advertising, science) rated by judges (CAT-style), with the six resources measured by separate instruments, then regressed product creativity on the resource battery. My recollection is that the resource composite predicted rated creativity at a moderate level, and that domain-specificity of the creativity ratings was itself a finding — but **I could not verify the specific R², beta weights, or N this session. `[UNANSWERED]`** Do not cite numbers for this study without checking the chapter.

**Critiques `[T]`:**
- The economic metaphor does the descriptive work but is not a mechanism. "Buy low, sell high" describes market timing in the field; it does not specify a process inside the person, and it is close to unfalsifiable post hoc — any accepted contribution can be redescribed as having been bought low.
- The six resources are largely repackaged individual-difference constructs; critics (including domain-specificity researchers like Baer) argue the theory inherits general-creativity assumptions that the CAT literature's own domain-specificity findings undercut.
- The "environment" resource is the systems model smuggled in as one variable among six. Csikszentmihalyi's position is that the field is not a resource the person possesses — it is the locus of the attribution itself. Investment theory keeps the person as the unit of analysis and treats the social system as a moderator; the systems model denies that this is coherent.
- Empirical follow-up by other labs testing all six resources jointly is thin. **`[UNANSWERED]`** — I could not survey replication attempts this session.

## 3. Amabile's componential model `[T]`

- Amabile, T. M. (1983). "The social psychology of creativity: A componential conceptualization." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 45*(2), 357–376.
- Amabile, T. M. (1983). *The Social Psychology of Creativity*. Springer-Verlag.
- Amabile, T. M. (1996). *Creativity in Context: Update to The Social Psychology of Creativity*. Westview Press.
- Amabile, T. M., & Pratt, M. G. (2016). "The dynamic componential model of creativity and innovation in organizations: Making progress, making meaning." *Research in Organizational Behavior, 36*, 157–183.

**Three components:**

1. **Domain-relevant skills** — factual knowledge, technical skill, special talents in the domain. The raw material; sets the ceiling on what the person *can* produce.
2. **Creativity-relevant processes** (in 1983, "creativity-relevant skills") — cognitive style favoring novel perspectives, heuristics for generating novel ideas, conducive work style, breaking perceptual set, tolerance for ambiguity.
3. **Task motivation** — attitude toward the task and perception of one's own reason for undertaking it.

**The intrinsic motivation principle** (1983, and the organizing claim of *Creativity in Context*): people are most creative when they feel motivated primarily by the interest, enjoyment, satisfaction, and challenge of the work itself, rather than by external pressures. Extrinsic constraints — expected evaluation, surveillance, contracted-for reward, competition, restricted choice — were shown across a program of experiments to undermine creativity.

**The softening (1996).** In *Creativity in Context* Amabile revised the principle to admit **synergistic extrinsic motivators**. The revised statement: intrinsic motivation is conducive to creativity; controlling extrinsic motivation is detrimental; but **informational or enabling extrinsic motivation** can be conducive, particularly when initial intrinsic motivation is high, and particularly for stages of the process that are not idea-generation. Recognition that confirms competence or supplies information about the work, or resources that enable deeper engagement, do not carry the cost that surveillance and contracted reward do. This is a real retreat from the 1983 position and is often under-reported.

**Amabile & Pratt (2016) revision — the dynamic componential model.** Principal changes `[T]`:
- Adds **meaningfulness of the work** and **affect** as explicit drivers, alongside motivation.
- Adds **progress** (from the Progress Principle work with Kramer) as a dynamic loop: small wins feed inner work life, which feeds subsequent creativity — the model becomes cyclical rather than a stage sequence.
- Recasts the individual model into a **multi-level** model where individual creativity and organizational innovation are nested, with organizational components (resources, management practices, motivation to innovate) paralleling the individual triad.
- Retains the componential structure but abandons the strict linear stage model of the creative process.

**Relevance to your problem:** Amabile's own theory locates creativity in the person and process; her *measurement* instrument (the CAT) locates it in the field. This tension is not an accident of her career — the CAT exists because she needed a criterion for the componential experiments and concluded that no non-social criterion was available.

## 4. Gruber and Simonton — where the measurement is taken `[T]`

**Gruber's evolving systems approach.** Gruber, H. E. (1974/1981). *Darwin on Man: A Psychological Study of Scientific Creativity* (2nd ed., University of Chicago Press); Gruber, H. E., & Davis, S. N. (1988), "Inching our way up Mount Olympus: The evolving-systems approach to creative thinking," in Sternberg (Ed.), *The Nature of Creativity*, pp. 243–270; Wallace, D. B., & Gruber, H. E. (Eds.) (1989), *Creative People at Work*, Oxford University Press.

Gruber's unit is the **single creative person over a lifetime**, studied as a system of three loosely coupled evolving subsystems: **knowledge**, **purpose** (an organization of goals, or "network of enterprise"), and **affect**. The method is the idiographic case study of primary documents — Darwin's notebooks, day by day. Measurement here is on the *person's developing work*, and explicitly rejects both psychometric aggregation and the field's verdict as the object of study. Gruber's contribution to your question is negative but useful: it is the strongest existing case that something real and analyzable is happening in the individual system *independently* of whether a field ever ratifies it.

**Simonton's chance-configuration and historiometry.** Simonton, D. K. (1988), "Scientific genius: A psychology of science" (Cambridge UP) and "Creativity, leadership, and chance," in Sternberg (Ed.), *The Nature of Creativity*, pp. 386–426; Simonton, D. K. (1999), "Creativity as blind variation and selective retention: Is the creative process Darwinian?", *Psychological Inquiry, 10*(4), 309–328; Simonton, D. K. (2004), *Creativity in Science*, Cambridge UP.

Simonton's unit is the **product and the historical record**. Chance-configuration / BVSR: creators generate largely blind variations (mental elements combining into configurations), and selection — internal then social — retains the few that stabilize. His empirical instrument is **historiometry**: quantitative analysis of eminent creators using archival indicators (productivity counts, citation and reference concentration, encyclopedia space, longevity of reputation). Creativity is therefore measured *on the social system's residue*, at a lag of decades to centuries. His equal-odds rule (quality is a probabilistic function of quantity; hit rate does not rise with eminence) is the best-known operational output.

One Simonton construct is directly load-bearing for you, and I verified it in secondary use `[V]`: his **hierarchy of domains by peer-evaluation consensus** (Simonton, 2004, 2009), ranking physics > chemistry > biology > psychology > sociology, with arts and humanities lowest. Kaufman et al. (2013) build Study 2 on exactly this, extrapolating that engineering sits high because ABET/INCOSE codify the body of knowledge, whereas creative writing has no comparable gate. `[V]`

**The three positions, side by side:**

| Approach | Unit of measurement | Creativity is a property of |
|---|---|---|
| Csikszentmihalyi (systems) | field verdict on a domain variation | the system; attribution |
| Amabile (CAT, operationally) | product, judged by appropriate observers | the judgment |
| Gruber (evolving systems) | the person's work over a lifetime | the individual system |
| Simonton (historiometric) | archival trace of products and reputations | the product, revealed by long-run social selection |
| Sternberg & Lubart | person's resource profile + rated product | the person, moderated by environment |

## 5. The propulsion model — and whether anyone has operationalized it

- Sternberg, R. J. (1999). "A propulsion model of types of creative contributions." *Review of General Psychology, 3*(2), 83–100. `[V]` (seven types in the original) — [DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.3.2.83](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/1089-2680.3.2.83)
- Sternberg, R. J., Kaufman, J. C., & Pretz, J. E. (2001). "The propulsion model of creative contributions applied to the arts and letters." *Journal of Creative Behavior, 35*(2), 75–101. `[V]` — eight types; [ERIC EJ629421](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ629421)
- Sternberg, R. J., Kaufman, J. C., & Pretz, J. E. (2002). *The Creativity Conundrum: A Propulsion Model of Kinds of Creative Contributions*. Psychology Press. `[V]` — [publisher page](https://www.routledge.com/The-Creativity-Conundrum-A-Propulsion-Model-of-Kinds-of-Creative-Contributions/Sternberg-Kaufman-Pretz/p/book/9780415647090)
- Sternberg, R. J. (2003). "A propulsion model of creative leadership." *The Leadership Quarterly, 14*(4–5), 455–473. `[V]`

**The eight types**, grouped by relation to the existing paradigm `[V]` for the list; grouping `[T]`:

*Paradigm-preserving (accept current domain, accept its direction):*
1. **Replication** — shows the field is where it should be; keeps it there.
2. **Redefinition** — same place, seen from a new point of view.
3. **Forward incrementation** — moves the field forward in the direction it is already going.
4. **Advance forward incrementation** — moves it forward *further and faster* than it is ready for; often rejected in its own time.

*Paradigm-rejecting (reject current direction):*
5. **Redirection** — moves the field in a new direction from its current point.
6. **Reconstruction / redirection** — moves back to a past point, then forward in a different direction from there.
7. **Reinitiation** — starts the field over from a new point entirely.

*Paradigm-integrating:*
8. **Synthesis / integration** — merges two previously distinct domains or bodies of work.

Sternberg later recasts these as three kinds: paradigm-preserving, paradigm-defying, paradigm-integrating. `[V]`

### Operationalization status: essentially none

**Finding: I found no formal coding scheme with published interrater reliability (kappa, ICC, alpha) developed by Sternberg, Kaufman, and Pretz or by others applying the propulsion taxonomy to a corpus of real contributions.** `[V]` — searched; the model appears in the literature as a theoretical taxonomy demonstrated through illustrative examples (the 2001 arts-and-letters paper works by exemplar, not by independent coding), plus a leadership application (2003) and a further application paper (Sternberg & Kaufman, 2012). The model's own structure resists coding: the types are asserted to be qualitatively distinct but each admits quantitative variation (a forward incrementation may be a small step or a large leap `[V]`), and assignment of a type requires a judgment about the field's *direction of travel* at the time of the contribution — a judgment that is itself a field attribution.

I did not exhaustively search domain-specific secondary applications (music, film, innovation management) for kappa statistics. **`[UNANSWERED]`** — a targeted search per domain could still turn up an isolated coding study, but the absence of any such study in the model's own citation neighborhood after 25 years is itself informative.

**Consequence for you:** if you want a taxonomy of *kinds* of creative contribution rather than a scalar, the propulsion model is the most-cited candidate and it comes with **zero psychometric warrant**. Using it means building the coding scheme and reliability evidence yourself, from scratch.

## The rater-expertise evidence base — actual numbers

This is the part I verified line by line against primary PDFs, because the secondary summaries circulating on the web are **wrong**. A web search summary in this session confidently reported the poetry expert–novice correlation as .71 and novice alpha as .53. Both numbers belong to the *short story* study, not the poetry study. Here is the corrected ledger.

### Kaufman, Baer, Cole & Sexton (2008) — poetry `[V]`
*Creativity Research Journal, 20*(2), 171–178. DOI 10.1080/10400410802059929.

Design: 205 SciFaiku poems written in 10 minutes by 205 college students. Rated 1–6 for creativity, independently, no rubric.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Expert raters | n = 10 published poets |
| Novice raters | n = 106 college students (102 after cleaning) |
| Expert mean rating | M = 3.09, SD = 0.90 |
| Novice mean rating | M = 4.47, SD = 0.87 |
| Mean difference | t(407) = 15.64, p < .001, 95% CI [1.20, 1.55] — **experts rate much harsher** |
| **Expert–novice correlation** | **r(202) = .216, p = .003** (4.41% shared variance) |
| Expert coefficient α (10 raters) | .832 |
| Novice coefficient α (all raters) | .935 |
| **Spearman-Brown corrected to 10 raters: experts** | **.804** |
| **Spearman-Brown corrected to 10 raters: novices** | **.575** |

Authors' verdict, verbatim: *"it is far too low… to suggest that nonexpert ratings can be substituted for those of experts without changing the outcome. To make such a substitution would produce very different results."*

### The pattern that matters

1. **Novices are not a cheap expert.** In every study, small novice panels are unreliable (α ≈ .53–.66, sometimes near zero) and their rank orders diverge from experts' (r ≈ .22–.72). Large novice panels achieve high α — but high α among novices only proves *novices share a metric*, and Kaufman et al. state flatly for poetry: *"Whatever one might argue was the basis of the novices' judgments, it was not creativity in poetry as understood by experts in the field."* `[V]`
2. **Where novices fail worst:** (a) **poetry** (r = .22) worse than short stories (r = .71) — the gap widens as the domain becomes less popularly accessible `[V]`; (b) **highly codified/high-consensus domains** — engineering overall creativity r = .29, and even *quasi*-experts collapse to .52 there `[V]`; (c) **abstract vs. figurative art** (Hekkert & van Wieringen, 1996: experts and novices far apart on abstract, quasi-experts in between) `[V]`; (d) **children's music composition** (Hickey, 2001: children's ratings correlated with neither theorists' nor teachers') `[V]`; (e) **theoretically loaded dimensions** — "genesis" (does this open new perspectives on the problem?) produced r = .02 between experts and quasi-experts. That is the dimension closest to Csikszentmihalyi's actual construct (does it change the domain?), and it is the one where non-experts carry **literally no signal**. `[V]`
3. **Related benchmarks** `[V]`: film critics vs. IMDb users r = .72; IMDb users vs. students r = .65; **critics vs. students r = .43** (Plucker, Holden & Neustadter, 2008; Plucker, Kaufman, Temple & Qian, 2009). Gifted novices (high schoolers invested in the domain) reach r = .78 poetry / .77 stories with experts (Kaufman, Gentile & Baer, 2005) — i.e., *interest in the domain* buys most of what expertise buys, in low-consensus domains.
4. **Domain expertise beats eminence.** Kaufman et al. (2008) `[V]`: *"Experts in Big-C may not be the most appropriate judges of little-c… judges for an elementary school science fair need some scientific expertise, but Nobel laureates in physics might be less appropriate raters than science teachers."* Expertise must match **the population of products**, not just the domain.

## FIT / COST VERDICT: blind LLM graders as a synthetic field

### The structural claim

Under the systems model, a panel of LLM graders scoring artifacts blind is **not measuring creativity and is not approximating a measurement of creativity**. It is **constituting a field** — an artificial one — and emitting that field's attributions. This is not a criticism; it is a description that changes what you are permitted to claim.

Once you accept the framing, several things that look like methodological problems stop being problems, and several things that look fine stop being fine.

### What this buys you

**1. Your reliability statistics stop lying to you.** The single most useful transfer from this literature is Kaufman et al.'s warning that high α proves only that raters share a metric `[V]`. Multi-sample LLM grading trivially produces high α — same weights, same prompt, correlated errors. Under the CAT framing that looks like validity. Under the systems framing it is **exactly the novice-panel result**: 106 novices reached α = .94 on poetry while correlating .22 with the domain. **Report α if you want, but treat a high LLM-panel α as evidence of nothing at all about domain validity.** This one correction is worth more than any positive finding below.

**2. You get a principled sampling design for your grader panel.** Csikszentmihalyi's model says the field is heterogeneous and its composition determines the verdict. That converts "which prompt/model/persona should the grader use?" from a tuning nuisance into a **specification of which field you are simulating**. Deliberately varying grader persona, and treating between-persona variance as *construct-relevant variance rather than noise*, is the systems-model-correct design. Averaging it away is the systems-model-incorrect design.

**3. You get a legitimacy argument for panels over single graders — but only the weak version.** Amabile's operational definition licenses "creative = what appropriate observers independently agree is creative." An LLM panel can satisfy *independent* and *agree*. It cannot, without evidence, satisfy **appropriate**. Which brings us to the costs.

**4. You get a cheap, defensible research-grade instrument for relative comparison in low-consensus domains — if and only if you calibrate.** The Kaufman line "possibly high enough for group comparisons in research, certainly not acceptable for any kind of high-stakes individual assessment" (about r = .71) is the correct ceiling to aim at and the correct disclaimer to attach. `[V]`

**5. You get an unambiguous methodological precedent for what calibration must look like.** Amabile's 1983 collage study is the cautionary template: 14 novices reached ρ = .93 and *were never compared to experts* — so the agreement meant nothing `[V]`. Do not repeat this. The required evidence is a **correlation between your LLM panel and a human expert panel, on your artifacts, in your domain**. Nothing substitutes for it.

### What it costs — and what you cannot claim

**1. You cannot claim the panel measures creativity as understood by the domain, absent a human-expert correlation.** The blind LLM panel is, structurally, the novice panel: high internal agreement, unknown relation to the field that owns the domain. The base rates from the human literature say the honest prior on your uncalibrated expert-correspondence is **somewhere in .2 to .7 depending on domain**, not .9. Claiming otherwise without measurement is unwarranted.

**2. The cost is domain-dependent and predictably so — and it is worst exactly where you probably want to use it.**

| Your artifact domain | Expected LLM-as-novice-field penalty | Evidence base |
|---|---|---|
| Popularly accessible, low-consensus (short stories, ad copy, general prose) | **Moderate.** Human novices reached .71–.72 there. An LLM trained on vast domain text plausibly sits at quasi-expert level (.77–.89 range) | Kaufman et al. 2009, 2013 Study 1 `[V]` |
| Esoteric, low-consensus (poetry, abstract art, avant-garde form) | **Severe.** Human novices reached .22. Expert standards here are internalized, non-verbalizable, and anti-popular by construction | Kaufman et al. 2008 `[V]`; Hekkert & van Wieringen 1996 `[V]` |
| Highly codified, high-consensus (engineering, math, physics, security, systems design) | **Severe.** Novices .29, *quasi-experts* .52 on overall creativity. Certified expertise is the gate and there is no shortcut | Kaufman et al. 2013 Study 2 `[V]`; Simonton domain hierarchy `[V]` |

Note the trap: engineering-type domains have the **most consensus among experts** — which people mistake for "easy to judge." It is the opposite. High consensus means a real, codified standard exists that non-members do not have. The expert–quasi-expert correlation of .52 in a domain where experts agree at α = .86 is the whole lesson.

**3. Decomposed rubrics do not save you, and one dimension actively betrays you.** In Study 2, `relevance/effectiveness` transferred well (experts × novices = .79) while `genesis` did not (experts × quasi-experts = **.02**) `[V]`. The pattern is legible: **dimensions reducible to functional criteria transfer to non-expert judges; dimensions requiring a model of the domain's current frontier do not.** Since "does this open new perspectives on the problem" is the operationalization closest to Csikszentmihalyi's actual definition (a variation that changes the domain), your LLM panel is likely most trustworthy on the dimensions least related to creativity and least trustworthy on the one that *is* creativity. **Do not report a composite that averages these together.** A composite hides the .02.

**4. LLM panel members are not independent in the way the CAT requires.** Amabile's definition requires *independent* agreement — the independence is what makes the agreement informative. Samples from one model share weights; samples across frontier models share pretraining corpora, RLHF conventions, and a documented preference for fluent, hedged, structurally conventional text. Correlated bias inflates α without adding information. Systems-model translation: **your synthetic field is a single homogeneous clique, not a field.** A field with one opinion has no selection pressure. Cross-model panels help; they do not fix this.

**5. The frontier problem is fatal to the strongest claim, and no calibration fixes it.** Csikszentmihalyi's construct is *change to the domain*. Judging that requires knowing where the domain currently is. An LLM's domain model is (a) frozen at a training cutoff, (b) weighted toward the well-represented center of the distribution, and (c) reproduced by generation from the same distribution it is judging. Csikszentmihalyi's own temporal caveat applies with force: fields revise their verdicts, and "advance forward incrementation" (Sternberg et al.'s type 4) is *defined* as the contribution the contemporaneous field rejects `[V]`. Your synthetic field is a conservative, retrospective, center-of-mass field. **It will systematically undervalue exactly the class of work the theory says matters most.** No amount of expert-correlation calibration on ordinary artifacts detects this, because the failure is in the tail.

**6. You cannot use the propulsion model to fix it.** The obvious repair — score contributions by *type* rather than degree — has no psychometric infrastructure. Twenty-five years, no coding scheme, no reliability data `[V]`. Building one is a research project, not a feature.

### The claim you can defensibly make

> "Panel *P* — [k independent samples across models *M₁…Mₙ*, personas *p₁…pₖ*, prompt *X*] — assigned artifact *A* a creativity rating of *r*. On a calibration set of *N* artifacts in this domain, this panel correlates *ρ* with a human expert panel of *m* domain experts. Panel internal consistency was α, which we report as a homogeneity statistic and not as evidence of validity. Ratings are intended for relative comparison between artifacts of the same type produced under the same prompt, not for absolute or high-stakes individual assessment. The panel's judgments reflect the standards of a synthetic field constituted by the models and personas listed; a different panel specification would yield a different and equally legitimate ordering, per Amabile (1982) and Csikszentmihalyi (1988, 1999)."

### The claims you cannot make

- "This measures creativity." (No: it measures one field's attribution.)
- "High inter-rater agreement validates the scores." (Directly refuted: Kaufman et al. 2008, p. 175 `[V]`; Amabile 1983 collage precedent `[V]`.)
- "The panel is expert because the models are knowledgeable." Amabile's *appropriate observers* requires familiarity with the domain **and** — per Kaufman et al. — with the population of products being judged `[V]`. A model with encyclopedic knowledge of Nobel-level physics may be the wrong judge of a science-fair project for exactly the reason Kaufman gives.
- "It generalizes across domains." The human evidence says the expert–non-expert gap swings from .22 to .89 by domain. Calibrate per domain or claim nothing.
- "It can identify a breakthrough." Strongest cost. The synthetic field is structurally biased against advance forward incrementation and reinitiation.

### Minimum viable protocol

1. **Calibrate per domain** against a human expert panel. Report ρ. Without this, report nothing.
2. **Never report a composite that averages functional and frontier dimensions.** Score `effectiveness` / `craft` separately from `novelty` / `genesis` and report ρ per dimension. Expect them to diverge by an order of magnitude.
3. **Report α as homogeneity, explicitly disclaimed as non-validity**, with the Kaufman citation.
4. **Specify the panel as a field**: models, personas, prompt, sampling temperature, date. Treat it as part of the construct definition, versioned like a schema.
5. **Treat between-persona variance as signal.** Report its dispersion. A panel with no disagreement is a panel with no selection pressure.
6. **Ceiling your claims at "group comparisons in research."** Never individual, never high-stakes.
7. **Assume the tail is invisible.** Anything your panel ranks low may be an advance forward incrementation. Route low-consensus/high-variance outliers to a human, rather than discarding them.
