# Human Psychometric Creativity Testing: Divergent Thinking

## 1. Guilford: the origin of the four scores

J.P. Guilford's 1950 APA presidential address ("Creativity," *American Psychologist*, 5, 444–454) launched the field by observing that under 0.2% of psychological abstracts to that date touched creativity. His Structure of Intellect (SOI) model (Guilford, 1956, 1967, *The Nature of Human Intelligence*) crossed operations × contents × products into 120 (later 150, then 180) cells. **Divergent production** was one operation cell-block, defined as generating multiple answers where convergent production generates one.

The fluency / flexibility / originality / elaboration scheme comes directly from SOI cells, not from empirical factor recovery of a creativity construct:

- **Fluency** — count of admissible responses
- **Flexibility** — number of distinct semantic categories spanned
- **Originality** — statistical infrequency in the sample
- **Elaboration** — amount of detail added

Two structural problems were baked in at birth and have never been fixed. First, **originality and flexibility are arithmetically dependent on fluency**: produce more responses and you mechanically accrue more rare ones and more categories. Second, **frequency-based originality is sample-relative** — a response unique in n=50 is common in n=5,000, so scores are not comparable across studies, cohorts, or countries. Both problems drive essentially everything in sections 3 and 4 below.

## 2. TTCT: the famous instrument, and what's actually contested

E. Paul Torrance built the TTCT (1966; renormed 1974, 1984, 1990, 1998, 2008) on Guilford's scheme. Two batteries: **Verbal** (Thinking Creatively with Words, 7 activities, e.g. Ask-and-Guess, Product Improvement, Unusual Uses) and **Figural** (Thinking Creatively with Pictures, 3 activities: Picture Construction, Picture Completion, Lines/Circles). Roughly 30 minutes each, group-administered, hand-scored against a norms manual.

**Streamlined scoring (1984).** Torrance himself concluded flexibility was redundant with fluency on the Figural form and *removed it*. The streamlined Figural scores five norm-referenced dimensions — Fluency, Originality, Elaboration, **Abstractness of Titles**, **Resistance to Premature Closure** — plus 13 criterion-referenced "creative strengths" checklist indicators, aggregated into a **Creativity Index**. Elaboration was dropped from the Verbal form because untrained raters could not score it reliably. Note what this means: the two scores dropped over 40 years of use are precisely the two that a naive implementer is most likely to reinvent.

**Reliability (the strong part).** Inter-rater reliability with *manual-trained* scorers is genuinely high — the technical manual reports median inter-rater r as high as .97 for the Verbal form, and Cropley (2000) reports both forms routinely above .90. Kim (2006, "Can We Trust Creativity Tests?", *Creativity Research Journal*, 18, 3–14) puts trained-scorer agreement above 90% but stresses this is conditional on close manual adherence; untrained scoring is markedly worse. Even with high rank-order agreement, **self-trained raters show significant mean-level differences** — same ordering, different calibration — so scores are not comparable across scoring shops. Internal consistency (KR-21) for the Creativity Index centers around **.90 by grade, .89 by age**; technical-manual internal consistencies generally sit in the .80s–low .90s. Acar, Lee & Scherer (2024, *European Journal of Psychological Assessment*), a reliability generalization over 44 correlation matrices from 38 studies (N = 13,108), found composite ω = **.81** overall, but decomposed into an **Innovative factor ω = .85** and an **Adaptive factor ω = .62** — the latter is not adequate for individual decisions, and the two-factor model beat the unidimensional model. Test-retest is thinner; a Persian Form-A study (N = 1,015) reported alphas of .751 (fluency), .812 (flexibility), .766 (originality), .832 (elaboration), .784 (total).

**Predictive validity (the contested part).** Report these numbers together or you will be misled by any one of them:

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Torrance's own longitudinal follow-ups | Fluency/flexibility/originality predictive coefficients **.34–.48**, above IQ, HS achievement, peer nomination (**.09–.37**) |
| Plucker (1999, *Creativity Research Journal*) reanalysis of Torrance's data | DT explained ~**half** the variance in adult creative achievement; >3× the variance explained by IQ |
| Kim (2008, *Journal of Creative Behavior*, 42, 106–130) meta-analysis, 274 coefficients | DT → creative achievement **r = .22**; IQ → creative achievement **r = .17**; TTCT specifically **r = .33** (p < .0001), best of the instruments included; ~51.8% of coefficients came from the TTCT |
| Kim (2005) | DT ↔ IQ **r = .17** — DT is largely distinct from g |
| **Runco, Millar, Acar & Cramond (2010,** *Creativity Research Journal*, 22, 361–368**), 50-year follow-up** | TTCT correlated **moderately with *personal* achievement, not significantly with *public* achievement**. The four-index composite showed a **significant quadratic** trend with personal achievement (i.e. an *optimum*, not monotone). Only the **IQ × creativity interaction** predicted public achievement. A composite of "Beyonder" traits (Love of work, Tolerance of mistakes, Minority of one) outpredicted both IQ and DT |

**Be blunt: the headline is weaker than the reputation.** Kim's own meta-analytic r = .22 for DT generally is a ~5% variance ceiling; the TTCT's r = .33 is ~11%. Plucker's "half the variance" and the 50-year follow-up's null-for-public-achievement cannot both be the takeaway, and the 50-year study is the one with the longest criterion horizon and the authors closest to Torrance. The honest summary is: **DT scores carry real but modest signal for everyday/personal creative behavior, and the evidence for predicting eminent or publicly recognized achievement is weak and non-linear.**

**The "creativity crisis" is, as far as the best available reanalysis goes, not real.** Kim (2011, *Creativity Research Journal*, 23, 285–295) analyzed TTCT renorming data and reported creative-thinking scores static or declining from 6th grade on, and declining since 1990 even as IQ rose (Flynn effect) — amplified by Bronson & Merryman's 2010 *Newsweek* cover. **Barbot, Hass & Reiter-Palmon (2021, "Is There Really a Creativity Crisis? A Critical Review and Meta-Analytic Re-Appraisal," *Journal of Creative Behavior*, doi:10.1002/jocb.483)** re-ran cross-temporal meta-analyses on **over 330,000 participants** across TTCT norming editions and found **no evidence of a generational decline**, attributing Kim's result to problematic sampling decisions, statistical approach, and axis-scaled data visualization (their rescaling to 95% population coverage flattens the "decline" visually). They call the crisis "a counter-productive societal and scientific myth." Treat any citation of the creativity crisis as a red flag for the citing work's rigor.

## 3. Alternate Uses Task: how modern research actually scores it

The AUT ("list unusual uses for a brick") is the workhorse. Modern practice has largely **abandoned frequency-based originality** because of the fluency confound and sample-relativity described in §1. The alternatives, from Silvia et al. (2008, "Assessing Creativity With Divergent Thinking Tasks: Exploring the Reliability and Validity of New Subjective Scoring Methods," *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*, 2, 68–85):

- **Average scoring** — raters score *every* response; person score = mean. Highest reliability, highest cost.
- **Top-2 scoring** — the *participant* nominates their two best ideas; only those are rated. Kills the fluency confound by construction (everyone contributes exactly 2 rated items). Silvia's generalizability analyses put person-variance at **56.2%** (unusual uses) and **50.0%** (instances) — at least half, but **slightly less dependable than average scoring**; Silvia (2011) confirms Top-2 is generally *less* reliable than average scoring. It buys discriminant validity at a reliability cost.
- **Snapshot scoring** — rater gives one holistic score to the participant's whole response set. Hass, Rivera & Silvia (2018, *Frontiers in Psychology*, PMC6099101), with MTurk raters, found it **difficult to achieve adequate reliability with snapshot**, while average-rating achieved good reliability on both task families at a feasible items × raters combination. **Snapshot is the cheap option that does not work.**
- **Benedek et al. (2013,** *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts***)** extended top-scoring: originality showed good internal consistency, rising with number of top-ideas and time-on-task, but **convergent validity peaked at ~2–3 minutes on task and ~3 top ideas** — more is not better.
- **Forthmann et al., "Don't Throw the 'Bad' Ideas Away!"** — Maximum and Top-2 scoring can *exceed* average scoring in measurement precision **when the bad ideas' originality is used as auxiliary information**; recommendation is to retain all ideas at scoring time.

Actual inter-rater reliabilities in the field are far below the TTCT manual's .90s. From the nine datasets federated in Organisciak et al. (see §4), **ICC(2,k) ranges .48 to .85** across published AUT studies:

| Dataset | Prompts | Raters | n responses | ICC2k |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silvia et al. 2008 | brick, knife | 3 | 3,425 | **.48** |
| Hofelich Moer 2016 | paperclip, brick | 4 | 3,843 | .67 |
| Silvia et al. 2017 | box, rope | 3 | 2,272 | .67 |
| Silvia et al. 2009 | brick, knife, box | 4 | 4,099 | .69 |
| MOTES-F | 8 | 4 | 2,924 | .73 |
| Beaty & Silvia 2012 | brick | 3 | 1,807 | .72 |
| MOTES-P | 10 | 4 | 339 | .81 |
| Beaty et al. 2018 | box, rope | 4 | 2,918 | .81 |
| Dumas et al. 2020 | 10 | 3 | 5,435 | .85 |

Two causes of the low end are documented: raters are typically undergraduates scoring thousands of responses, and **fatigue degrades reliability**; and unlike the Consensual Assessment Technique, most studies do not have raters reconcile disagreements because reconciliation is expensive.

**The empirical human ceiling.** Organisciak et al. measured it directly: across randomized pairings of judges on duplicated responses, **human-to-human r = .83**; a single judgment against the less-noisy mean of all judgments of its duplicates, **r = .88**. This is the number that matters for any automated system — .88 is the approximate ceiling, not 1.0.

## 4. Automated scoring: the real numbers

### SemDis (Beaty & Johnson, 2021, *Behavior Research Methods*, 53, 757–780)

Open platform computing semantic distance between prompt and response over five semantic spaces (LSA/GloVe-family), with a latent factor from their common variance. Replicates the serial-order effect and shows convergent validity. **Its person-level correlations with human ratings are strong; its response-level correlations are weak.** Beaty et al. (2022, *Creativity Research Journal*, 34, 245–260, "Semantic Distance and the Alternate Uses Task: Recommendations for Reliable Automated Assessment of Originality") report the SemDis–human correlation as **moderate** and explicitly call for work on maximally reliable items. Beaty & Johnson also establish that semantic distance is **primarily a measure of novelty, divorced from usefulness** — it scores one half of the standard novelty × appropriateness definition of creativity.

Benchmarked against the largest multi-judged AUT corpus: **best SemDis model r = .12 overall (mean per-prompt .19)**; Open Creativity Scoring's clustering approach **r = .26 (mean per-prompt .31)**. One neuroimaging comparison is diagnostic of what the two things measure: visuospatial intelligence correlated **r = .46** with human creativity ratings but only **r = .11** with semantic distance.

### Ocsai (Organisciak, Acar, Dumas & Berthiaume, 2023, *Thinking Skills and Creativity*, 49, 101356)

Fine-tuned T5 and GPT-3 on human-judged AUT responses. Corpus: **27,217 responses from 2,039 participants across 9 datasets**; after removing 7,015 exact duplicates (25.8% of data), **20,202 responses**. Deduplication is the methodological linchpin — without it a supervised model memorizes response→score lookups and the numbers are inflated (their non-deduplicated model hits r = .86, which they explicitly caution is leakage).

| System | Overall r vs. human | Mean per-prompt r |
|---|---|---|
| SemDis (best) | .12 | .19 |
| Open Creativity Scoring (clustering) | .26 | .31 |
| Fine-tuned T5 / GPT-3 (Ocsai) | **.76 – .81** | .19 – .80 range across prompts |
| Supervised average across configs | **.783** | — |
| Baseline semantic-distance average | .188 | — |
| **Human-human ceiling** | **.83 (.88 vs. de-noised mean)** | — |
| GPT-3 zero-shot | .13 | — |
| ChatGPT zero-shot | .19 | — |
| GPT-4 zero-shot | .53 | — |
| GPT-4, 5-shot | .66 | — |
| GPT-4, 20-shot | .70 | — |
| **Held-out (never-seen) prompts, best fine-tuned** | **.63** (.66 prompt-level) vs. baselines .14–.28 | — |

Three findings with direct engineering consequence:

1. **Data efficiency is extreme.** Fine-tuning beats semantic-distance baselines with **1% of training data**; at 5% (804 labels) babbage hits r = .61, ada r = .55. You do not need 27k labels — you need a few hundred *good* ones.
2. **Transfer to unseen prompts costs ~0.15–0.18 r** (.81 → .63). Automated DT scoring generalizes across items but not for free.
3. **Zero-shot LLM scoring is not competitive with fine-tuning but is now competitive with the entire semantic-distance literature.** GPT-4 zero-shot (.53) beats every unsupervised method by a factor of 2–4. This is the most operationally relevant number in this report.

**Stated failure modes** (their own, plus what the results imply):
- **Inexact duplicates survive deduplication** ("paperweight"/"paper weight" collapsed, but "use as a weight" and "weight to hold objects in place" did not) — part of the .81 is near-memorization.
- **The models may be learning rater-group idiosyncrasies**, not creativity: fine-tuned model prediction distributions hew far closer to the human distribution (by skew and KS D) than baselines do. Ground-truth skew 0.51. Fitting the rater panel's response style is a confound with fitting the construct.
- **Item difficulty is not what's driving performance**: entropy of human judgments for an item does **not** correlate with model performance on that item (r = .004). Nothing in the item's human-disagreement profile predicts where the model will fail — you cannot triage.
- Untested at publication: robustness to gaming/cheating, poor spelling, non-AUT DT tasks (instances, consequences), non-English (subsequently addressed — Polish GPT-3 scoring across N=195 and N=404, 6,121 ideas, reproduced serial-order effects and distinguished "be creative" from "be fluent" instructions).
- Domain-adaptive pretraining matters: for elementary-aged responses, a model pretrained on child-facing text beats a general model.

Current deployment: **OCSAI** at openscoring.du.edu, GPT-4o-mini supervised on ~27k human-rated AUT responses. Acar et al. (2023, *Gifted Child Quarterly*, 67, 3–17) applied automated originality scoring to the TTCT-Verbal.

## 5. Other instruments, briefly

**Remote Associates Test (Mednick, 1962, *Psychological Review*, 69, 220–232).** Three cue words, one word associatively linking all three (cottage / swiss / cake → cheese). This is **convergent**, not divergent — it has a single correct answer and is scored objectively. It measures associative reach under a correctness constraint, and it is a poor proxy for open-ended creativity precisely because it is closed-ended.

**Wallach & Kogan (1965, *Modes of Thinking in Young Children*).** Untimed, game-like, individually administered DT battery, deliberately stripped of test atmosphere. Their result — that under those conditions DT loads on a factor distinct from intelligence — is the origin of the "administration conditions change the construct" problem that still contaminates DT comparisons.

**Divergent Association Task (Olson, Nahas, Chmoulevitch, Cropper & Webb, 2021, *PNAS*, 118(25), e2022340118).** Name **10 nouns as different from each other as possible**; score = mean pairwise cosine distance over **GloVe 840B** embeddings (the public implementation scores the **first 7 valid words**, scaled ×100). N = **8,914**. Validity: correlations with the AUT and Bridge-the-Associative-Gap task were **at least as high as those measures' correlations with each other**, with DAT–AUT the strongest pairing (Studies 1A/1B); **test-retest over 2 weeks was high** (Study 1C). ~4 minutes, fully objective, no raters. **UNANSWERED: I could not retrieve the exact r values from Studies 1A/1B — PNAS returned 403 and the PMC mirror was CAPTCHA-gated. Treat "moderate to strong / as high as the intercorrelations" as the verified claim; do not quote a number.** Known weaknesses, per the authors and later work: it has **better face validity for originality than for appropriateness**, and scores may partly index **overinclusive thinking or schizotypy** rather than creativity. Replication in Chinese elementary students (Zhang et al., 2024, *Thinking Skills and Creativity*) found a much smaller effect: **DAT ↔ AUT originality r(322) = .191**. Constructional critiques (CXN-DAT) argue the bag-of-words framing misses that language is a network of symbolic schemas larger than words.

**Runco Ideational Behavior Scale (RIBS; Runco, Plucker & Lim, 2001, *Creativity Research Journal*).** Self-report of frequency of ideational behavior, built explicitly *as a criterion* for DT tests because Runco judged the usual criteria inappropriate. Reliable, usually two-factor; DT scores are the primary significant predictors of RIBS scores in regression. Runco (2014, *Journal of Creative Behavior*) added distractor, contraindicative, and lie scales and found **very little incremental validity** from them.

**Kaufman Domains of Creativity Scale (K-DOCS; Kaufman, 2012, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*).** Self-rated creativity across Self/Everyday, Scholarly, Performance, Mechanical/Scientific, Artistic. In at least one adolescent sample (N = 34, grades 7–11), **K-DOCS predicted DT performance while RIBS did not**.

**Creative Achievement Questionnaire (Carson, Peterson & Higgins, 2005, *Creativity Research Journal*, 17, 37–50).** Checklist of concrete accomplishments across 10 domains — the closest thing the field has to an objective real-world criterion. Test-retest **r = .81** (N = 117), **α = .96**. Predictive validity against artist-rated collages **r = .59** (N = 39; inter-artist R = .71; rising to **.65** with aesthetic appeal partialled). Convergent validity (N = 86): **DT r = .47**, Intellect **.51**, Creative Personality Scale **.33**, Openness **.33**. Factor structure is three-factor (Expressive, Performance, Scientific), which is a problem because nearly everyone computes a single total score.

**How well does self-report track performance?** CAQ↔DT at r = .47 is the strong end (~22% shared variance) and it is measured against DT, not against blind-rated products. Self-report is cheap, correlates with personality (Openness) about as much as with performance, and is structurally unavailable for scoring an artifact. For your use case it is informative only as evidence about *which construct labels humans can agree on*.

## 6. FIT / COST verdict for blind LLM-graded artifact evaluation

**What transfers — build these:**

1. **Separate novelty from appropriateness, and score them independently.** This is the single most load-bearing transfer. The DT literature's central failure is that semantic-distance methods measure novelty divorced from usefulness (Beaty & Johnson, 2021, stated explicitly), and the DAT's own authors concede weak appropriateness face validity. A grader that emits one "creativity" score inherits a 70-year-old confound. **FIT: high. COST: near zero** — two rubric dimensions instead of one.
2. **Fine-tuning (or few-shot with real graded exemplars) beats every clever unsupervised metric, and cheaply.** Ocsai's r = .783 vs. .188 is a 4× gap, and 1% of training data (~200–800 labels) already clears the baselines; GPT-4 at 20-shot reaches r = .70 with zero training. **FIT: very high. COST: low** — a few hundred human-graded artifacts, calibration exemplars in the prompt. **This should be the default and it should displace any plan to score creativity by embedding distance.**
3. **The .83/.88 human-agreement ceiling as the target metric.** Do not evaluate a grader against 1.0. Measure human-human agreement on your own artifact type first, then report grader-human r as a fraction of it. **FIT: high. COST: one duplicate-rating study.**
4. **Deduplicate before measuring grader accuracy.** Organisciak's .86-with-leakage vs. .81-deduplicated is the cautionary number, and near-duplicates survived their filter. If your eval set contains paraphrases of training artifacts, your grader's reported correlation is partly a lookup. **FIT: high. COST: low, and it is the cheapest way to avoid publishing a wrong number.**
5. **Average-over-all-items scoring, not holistic snapshot.** Snapshot scoring is the intuitive cheap design and Hass et al. (2018) showed it does not reach adequate reliability while average-rating does. If you are tempted to have one LLM call holistically rate a whole portfolio, the human literature says that is the design that fails. **FIT: high. COST: N× more grader calls — this is the real budget line.**
6. **Rank-order vs. calibration are different problems.** The TTCT's own finding — high inter-rater correlation, significant mean-level rater differences — means an LLM grader panel can be near-perfectly correlated and still miscalibrated. Report both. **FIT: high. COST: near zero.**
7. **Top-2 / participant-nominated scoring, adapted.** Asking a generator to nominate its own best k outputs and scoring only those removes the fluency confound by construction and mirrors real deployment (you ship one artifact, not twenty). Benedek's ~3-top-ideas optimum is a plausible starting k. **FIT: medium-high. COST: low, but note Silvia's finding that Top-2 is *less* reliable than average — use it for discriminant validity, not as your primary reliability driver, and follow Forthmann in retaining the unselected outputs as auxiliary information.**

**What does not transfer — do not build these:**

- **Frequency-based originality.** Sample-relative, fluency-contaminated, and non-comparable across runs. For LLM outputs it is worse than for humans: sampling temperature directly controls response frequency, so you would be measuring a decoding hyperparameter.
- **Fluency as a creativity score.** For a human, producing 20 uses for a brick is effortful and informative. For a model, it is a `max_tokens` setting. Fluency is not a creativity signal in machine-generated artifacts; it is a confound to be *controlled*, not scored.
- **Elaboration.** Torrance removed it from the Verbal form for un-scorable inter-rater reliability. For LLMs it is additionally degenerate — verbosity is free. Worse, there is an active line of work (arXiv 2604.20569) on elaboration *inflating* automated originality scores. If you score elaboration at all, score it as a control variable with length held constant.
- **Semantic distance as your primary metric.** r = .12 at response level against human judgment. It has an appealing mathematical story and it does not work.
- **TTCT norms, percentiles, and the Creativity Index.** Human-normed on schoolchildren across five renorming cohorts. There is no defensible mapping from a machine artifact to those norms.
- **DAT applied to model outputs.** It scores 7 nouns' mean pairwise GloVe cosine. Against a model, it measures decoding diversity and is trivially gameable by prompting for maximally unrelated nouns. It is a fine 4-minute human screener and a meaningless model benchmark.
- **All self-report instruments** (CAQ, RIBS, K-DOCS). Structurally inapplicable — there is no respondent. Their only use to you is as evidence on which construct facets humans can consistently self-locate.
- **RAT.** Convergent, single-correct-answer, and near-saturated by current models.

**The one deep structural warning.** Ocsai's most uncomfortable result is that the fine-tuned models' prediction distributions match the *human rater panel's* distribution far better than baselines do, and that item-level human-judgment entropy does not predict where the model errs (r = .004). The plausible reading is that a fine-tuned grader partly learns **the rater group's response style**, not the construct. In a blind-LLM-grading pipeline this compounds: you would be fitting an LLM to imitate a particular set of human raters, then treating its output as ground truth about creativity. Mitigations the literature supports: hold out *raters*, not just responses, when validating; hold out *prompts* and expect the ~.15–.18 r drop; and report distributional fit (skew, KS D) alongside correlation, because a grader can hit a high r while producing a badly shaped score distribution.

**Priority order if you build one thing:** a small set of human-graded exemplars driving a few-shot or fine-tuned grader, scoring novelty and appropriateness separately, averaged over items, evaluated against a measured human-human ceiling on deduplicated held-out prompts. Everything else in this literature is either superseded by that or is a documented dead end.
