# The Case Against Creativity Measurement: A Skeptic's Map

Everything below is sourced. Where I could not verify a number I say so explicitly. Verdicts are mine: **KILLS** (the method should not be used for the stated purpose), **WOUNDS** (usable only with named corrections), **QUALIFIES** (criticism is real but survivable).

---

## 1. Divergent thinking: the validity wars

### 1.1 The predictive record, in actual numbers

This is the load-bearing fact and it is worse than the field's marketing suggests.

| Meta-analysis | k | N | DT ↔ creative achievement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kim (2008), *J. Creative Behavior* | 27 studies (1958–2005) | 47,197 | **r = .216** |
| Said-Metwaly, Taylor, Camarda & Barbot (2022), *PACA* | 70 studies (1969–2020), 766 effect sizes | ~14,900 | **r = .18** |
| da Costa et al. (2015) | — | — | r = .27 (cited secondhand; I did not verify the primary) |

Kim's own comparison: IQ ↔ creative achievement was **r = .167**, so DT beats IQ by about five hundredths of a correlation. The updated, better-controlled meta-analysis moved the estimate *down*. At r = .18, divergent thinking explains roughly **3 percent** of the variance in real creative accomplishment. Torrance's own 40-year longitudinal follow-up (Cramond, Matthews-Morgan, Bandalos & Zuo, 2005) reports correlations of roughly .2–.3 with adult creative achievement — consistent with the meta-analytic picture, and routinely described in the field as "strong predictive validity," which it is not.

The honest framing: DT tests are weak-but-nonzero predictors, roughly on par with a personality facet. They are not measures of creativity in any sense that would let you rank two artifacts.

**Verdict: WOUNDS badly.** DT survives as a coarse population-level correlate of creative potential. It does not survive as an individual-level or artifact-level creativity score.

### 1.2 Baer's domain-specificity attack

John Baer's position across [The Case for Domain Specificity of Creativity](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/CaseforSpecificity.pdf) and [Domain Specificity and the Limits of Creativity Theory](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/Domains&Theory.pdf) is that DT tests measure *something*, but the something is domain-bound and therefore mislabeled.

Evidence he marshals:
- Baer (1993): creativity ratings of poems, mathematical puzzles, and collages by the same participants show **non-significant** cross-domain correlations, across age groups from elementary children to adults.
- DT scores and actual creative performance in three domains were unrelated even when both were collected in the same session.
- Training transfer is domain-bound: Baer (1996) trained middle-schoolers on poetry-relevant divergent thinking; blind expert judges rated their *poems* higher, but not their short stories. If DT were a general capacity, training should have generalized.

His sharpest specific point on the TTCT verbal form: high scores go to people who write the most and make the longest lists, so the test may be measuring skill at a particular kind of list-writing, or a cognitive style, and calling it creativity.

The complication: Diakidoy & Spanoudis (2002) ran the TTCT Verbal against a parallel domain-specific test and found *both* content-specific and task-specific factors mattered, which is a Sternberg-style "both/and" result rather than a clean win for specificity.

**Verdict: KILLS the general-creativity interpretation; QUALIFIES the tests themselves.** For your use case this is the decisive finding: there is no defensible way to take a domain-general DT score and treat it as a creativity measurement of a domain-specific artifact.

### 1.3 The fluency confound and its failed fixes

The structural problem: uniqueness and originality scores are arithmetically entangled with fluency (number of responses). Generate more, and you mechanically accumulate more unique/original hits. Much of the DT literature is therefore measuring *quantity* and reporting it as *quality*.

Attempted corrections, and why each is unsatisfying:

- **Ratio/average scoring** (originality ÷ fluency; Hocevar 1979, Plucker et al. 2011). Removes the artifactual correlation but introduces a strategy artifact: a person who produces many creative ideas *plus* a few dull ones scores worse than someone who produces only two good ones. Forthmann, Szardenings & Holling (2018) quantify the residual artifactual correlation and show ratio scores are not the clean fix they appear to be. Older validation work found flexibility/fluency ratios were "unreliable and invalid."
- **Subjective top-2 scoring** (Silvia et al. 2008): participants nominate their two best ideas, which are rated. Decorrelates from fluency and outperforms snapshot and average scoring — but discards most of the data, a loss Forthmann et al. flag directly. Benedek and colleagues find ~3 top ideas is the sweet spot.
- **Max-scoring** (Smeekens & Kane 2016), **first-three** (Clark & Mirels 1970): same tradeoff, arbitrary cutoffs.

**Verdict: WOUNDS.** There is no scoring rule that is simultaneously fluency-independent, information-preserving, and strategy-invariant. Every published DT correlation should be read with the question "which scoring rule, and does the finding survive the others?"

## 2. TTCT-specific critiques

### 2.1 The Baer–Kim exchange

Baer's ["How Divergent Thinking Tests Mislead Us"](https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/BaerTTCTDebate.pdf) (Division 10 debate, 2011) argues the longitudinal validity evidence is contaminated: the criterion measures are **self-report checklists**, and many of them involve making lists — exactly the task format of the predictor. Predictor and criterion share method variance by construction.

Kim's rebuttal ("Proven Reliability and Validity of the TTCT") is partly effective and partly evasive. Effective: much of what Baer cites concerns *other* DT tests (Wallach 1976 on ideational fluency, Simonton 2007 on the RAT, Han 2003 on Wallach-Kogan), and Baer (1993) used only the TTCT "Unusual Uses" item, not the instrument. Evasive: the reply that the criterion was "biographical information and a checklist of creative accomplishments" rather than self-rated creativity does not remove the self-report problem, it renames it.

### 2.2 Internal incoherence

The single most damaging TTCT number is rarely quoted: **Verbal and Figural forms correlate r = 0.06** (Cramond, 1994). Two subtests of the same instrument, sharing essentially no variance. Whatever "TTCT creativity" is, the test does not measure one of it. Consistent with this, several groups argue the figural test is better described by a two-factor innovative/adaptive structure aligned with Kirton's *cognitive style* theory — i.e., style, not ability.

The Flexibility dimension was **dropped from figural scoring in 1984** because of reliability problems. A recent reliability generalization finds Form A more reliable than Form B, and composite/INNOV scores more precise than ADAPT scores.

### 2.3 Norms and coaching

Norms have been refreshed (1974, 1984, 1990, 1998, 2008), so "stale norms" is a weaker charge than critics imply. But originality scoring is *statistical* — an answer is original if few people in the norm sample gave it — which means the construct is defined by a historical reference population and drifts with culture and with the composition of that population.

**Coaching/practice effects: UNANSWERED.** I could not locate a meta-analysis or well-powered study quantifying TTCT score gains from practice or coaching. The adjacent, well-established finding is the instruction effect (§6), which is arguably worse. Do not assume the TTCT is coaching-resistant; assume it is untested.

### 2.4 The "creativity crisis"

Kim (2011) claimed U.S. creativity scores declined from 1990 onward across ~272,600 K-12 and adult examinees in the six TTCT norming samples, with the sharpest decline in K-3.

The reappraisal — Said-Metwaly and colleagues, ["Is There Really a Creativity Crisis? A Critical Review and Meta-Analytic Re-Appraisal"](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1309936), *J. Creative Behavior* (2021) — used **over 330,000 participants** across the same norming editions and concluded the claim rested on "problematic empirical decisions, statistical approaches, data representation, and interpretation." Their cross-temporal meta-analyses found **no evidence of generational decline**. One of their most effective exhibits is simply Kim's figure replotted at a scale covering 95% of the population distribution, where the "crisis" flattens into noise.

Independently, Weinstein, Clark, DiBartolomeo & Davis (2014), [A Decline in Creativity? It Depends on the Domain](https://katiedavisresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/2014_CRJ-A-decline-in-creativity.pdf), examined actual adolescent artifacts (visual art, creative writing) over time and found domain-divergent trends, not a uniform decline.

**Verdict on the crisis claim: KILLED.** It is the highest-profile empirical claim in modern creativity research and it did not replicate. Treat it as a cautionary tale about y-axis scaling and standard-score arithmetic, not as a finding.

## 3. Rater-based measurement (CAT): what reliability hides

### 3.1 Reliability is not validity, and the CAT literature conflates them

The CAT's headline credential is inter-rater α of .80–.90 among experts. The structural critique — made most directly in a dissertation-length treatment and echoed by Cseh & Jeffries — is that **Amabile (1982) treated inter-rater reliability as construct validity**. There is abundant reliability evidence and comparatively little validity evidence. High agreement among judges tells you they share a standard; it tells you nothing about whether the shared standard tracks creativity. A panel of judges who all reward technical polish will agree beautifully and measure the wrong thing.

Early CAT applications were criticized on exactly this point: procedures often **failed to separate creativity from technical correctness and aesthetic appeal**.

### 3.2 Expert vs. novice: when they disagree, who is right?

- **Poetry** (Kaufman, Baer, Cole & Sexton, 2008, *CRJ*): 10 experts, 106 novices, 205 poems. Expert and novice ratings were *substantially different*; novice inter-rater reliability was low and their judgments did not match experts'.
- **Short fiction**: expert–novice correlation of only **r = .71**, with experts far more internally consistent.
- **Apparel design**: expert–novice **r = .83** for creativity, .78 for technical quality.

Note what this pattern actually implies: expert–novice convergence is **domain-dependent and unpredictable in advance**. And the field's answer to "who is right when they disagree" is circular — experts are right *because* they agree more, which is the reliability-as-validity move again. Kaufman's pragmatic compromise (quasi-experts) is a budget concession, not a validity argument.

### 3.3 Sample-relative scoring

Amabile's own guidelines instruct judges to rate **relativistically**, comparing items within the presented set rather than against absolute domain knowledge. This is a scaling decision with a large consequence: **CAT scores are not comparable across samples or studies.** A story rated 4.2 in one stimulus set is not the same object as a 4.2 in another.

Dollinger & Shafran (2005) attempted a fix by calibrating judges with anchor exemplars from a prior study; calibrated and uncalibrated means correlated .91, but the authors restricted the method to programmatic research where task and population are held fixed.

For an automated grading system this is the single most important CAT property to internalize: **the CAT is a ranking instrument masquerading as a rating instrument.**

### 3.4 Temporal instability of judges

Barth & Stadtmann (2021), [*Creativity Assessment over Time*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jocb.462), *JOCB* 55:396–409: 61 raters judged the same 90 fashion outfits twice, 2 or 4 weeks apart. Mean product ratings were extremely stable (consistency and agreement **ICCs > .90**); interval length didn't matter. But **individual raters drifted**. The aggregate is stable while the instrument producing it is not — fine if you always average many judges, fatal if you rely on a single judge or a small panel.

Corroborating from an adjacent domain: in an expert re-evaluation of research proposals ~3 months later, only **37.5%** of scores were unchanged, the remainder shifting by a point.

### 3.5 Procedural chaos

Cseh & Jeffries (2019), ["A scattered CAT"](https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/28402/), *PACA* 13(2):159–166: CAT implementations across the literature vary in judge expertise, criteria, instructions, and panel size without documentation, and researchers apply these choices without understanding their effects. Concretely: **panels above ~10 judges inflate the false-positive rate on inter-rater reliability; panels below 5 inflate both false positives and false negatives.** So published α values are not comparable across studies either.

### 3.6 Cultural instability of the standard

Comparing American and Chinese judges: American judges applied **stricter standards** for overall creativity, and **Chinese judges showed greater consensus** about what creativity is. Different populations of judges are running different functions with different variances.

**Verdict on the CAT: WOUNDS, but it remains the best of a bad field.** It is the only tradition that scores *artifacts* rather than proxies. Its scores are sample-relative, judge-panel-dependent, culture-dependent, and validated circularly. Use it as a within-batch ranking instrument with a large panel, never as an absolute score.

## 4. The criterion problem

Every validity claim in this field eventually points at "real creative achievement," which is itself a broken measuring stick.

**The CAQ** (Carson, Peterson & Higgins, 2005): α = .96, test–retest r = .81, r = .59 with artist ratings of a collage, r = .47 with DT. Impressive-looking. But:

- The distribution is **severely positively skewed by design** — Carson invokes Eysenck's (1993) claim that traits are normal but achievement is Poisson. Domain scores are **overdispersed with excessive zeros**; in adult samples the CAQ typically follows a **zero-inflated Poisson**.
- Silvia and colleagues' methodological point: conventional regression is *not* robust to this, and the field routinely runs OLS on it anyway. Correct handling requires count, censored, truncated, or ordinal models.
- The zeros are ambiguous. Censoring/floor effects mean people scoring zero genuinely differ from each other, and no model can recover that from the data.
- Benedek (2024) argues the right skew arises because creative potential predictors combine **multiplicatively**, not additively — simulations reproduce the empirical skew only under multiplicative interaction. If true, the entire linear-correlation literature between potential and achievement is misspecified, which would partly *explain* the r ≈ .18 ceiling.

**The deeper problem — confounding.** Achievement is opportunity, persistence, capital, network access, and publication norms as much as creative ability. Fraiberger et al. (*Science*, 2018), analyzing exhibition histories of half a million artists, show early access to prestigious institutions drives career trajectory via a path-dependent Markov process. Achievement measures partly encode institutional access.

**Circularity.** When DT is validated against the CAQ (self-report), and the CAT is validated against inter-rater agreement, and LLM judges are validated against the CAT, the chain never touches an external anchor. Baer's method-variance objection (predictor and criterion share the list-making format) is the concrete instance of a general disease.

**Verdict: KILLS the notion of a clean criterion.** There is no gold standard, only a network of mutually-validating proxies. Any system that claims to measure creativity "accurately" is claiming access to something the field does not have.

## 5. Bias and fairness

**Measurement invariance is mostly untested, and where tested it partly fails.**

- Kim, Dumas, Acar & Organisciak (2025), *Gifted Child Quarterly*: TTCT-Figural, N = 379 U.S. elementary students. The Innovative–Adaptive model reached configural, metric, and scalar invariance **across under- and over-represented ethnicities** — but the title states it is **not invariant across gender**. The instrument used for gifted identification does not measure the same construct in boys and girls.
- Cross-cultural DT: partial strong invariance held **only for real-world problem fluency**; no latent mean differences on that one indicator.
- The West–East meta-analysis (Barth & Stadtmann, 2024) reports a Western advantage of **g = 0.33, 95% CI [0.17, 0.49]** — and notes that **only 1 of 41 included studies tested measurement invariance**. An uninvariant comparison of latent means is not interpretable, so that g should be treated as descriptive of *test scores*, not of creativity.

**Do ratings encode conformity to the rater's culture? Partly yes, with a specific structure.** The theoretical claim (Leung & Morris 2010; Paletz & Peng 2008, *JCCP* 39:286–302) is that Western norms weight **novelty** while East Asian norms weight **usefulness/appropriateness and harmony**. McCarthy, Chen & McNamee (2018), [Novelty and Usefulness Trade-Off](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0022022116680479), *JCCP*, is the direct empirical test of the trade-off in idea evaluation. Implicit-belief work finds Americans valuing groundbreaking novelty and Chinese respondents valuing creativity *within constraints* — reworking tradition.

The most directly relevant finding for your use case, from a 2025 study comparing human and LLM assessors: both Eastern and Western assessors rated Eastern-created stories higher on **originality**, but on **appropriateness** assessors always preferred creators from **their own culture**. Appropriateness is where the in-group bias lives.

Field-level: creativity assessment research is conducted predominantly with white U.S. students and frequently does not report racial or ethnic composition at all.

**Verdict: WOUNDS.** Not a knockout for any single instrument, but it means any creativity score is partly a measure of match between artifact conventions and rater conventions, and the appropriateness half of the standard definition is the biased half.

## 6. Reactivity, instructions, and Goodhart

This is the section that matters most for a benchmark.

**Instruction effects are enormous.** The 2023 meta-analysis ["The power of the 'be creative' instruction"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0023969023000760) reports:

| Outcome | Effect of "be creative" instruction |
|---|---|
| Creativity (overall) | **d = .69**, p < .001 |
| Originality | **d = .79**, p < .001 |
| Flexibility | d = .48, reported p > .1 |
| Fluency | d = .06, n.s. |

*(Caveat: a d = .48 reported as non-significant is odd and suggests few effect sizes or high heterogeneity in that cell. Treat the flexibility figure as unreliable.)*

Acar, Runco & Park (2020), *PACA* 14(1):39–49, [doi:10.1037/aca0000256](https://www.markrunco.com/articles/what-should-people-be-told-when-they-take-a-divergent-thinking-test-a-meta-analytic-review-of-explicit-instructions-for-divergent-thinking), summarized as: **"researchers get what they ask for"** — be-creative instructions raise sophistication, be-fluent instructions raise counts. **The exact Hedges' g values from Acar et al. (2020) are UNANSWERED**; I could not retrieve the paper body.

An effect of d ≈ 0.8 on originality from a single sentence of instruction is larger than most substantive effects in the entire creativity literature. A score that moves that much on framing is not measuring a stable property of the producer.

**Direct Goodhart evidence, and it is decisive for automated novelty metrics.** In the Divergent Association Task (10 nouns, scored as mean pairwise semantic distance), the metric by construction rewards novelty and never checks appropriateness. Consequence: **random or stochastic word generation attains DAT scores rivaling strong LLMs while producing nothing meaningful.** The metric has a trivial degenerate optimum.

Corroborating: on UnpredictaBench, NoveltyBench Distinct₁₀ **correlates negatively with utility (r = −0.21)**, and the model with the *highest* lexical diversity ranks near the *bottom* on usefulness.

The emerging fix in benchmark design (CreativityPrism decomposes quality/novelty/diversity; automated evaluators pair semantic entropy with a validated novelty judge) is the right shape: **any unpaired novelty metric has a degenerate optimum and will be found by an optimizer.**

**Verdict: KILLS unpaired novelty metrics as benchmark targets.** Not "wounds." If your grader scores semantic distance or uniqueness without a simultaneous appropriateness gate, an optimizing system will exploit it, and the exploit is not subtle — it is noise.

## 7. Statistical critiques and the modern psychometric response

**What's broken:**
- **Fluency-entangled scores** (§1.3) create artifactual correlations that propagate into every downstream analysis.
- **Zero-inflation and overdispersion** in achievement criteria (§4) violate the assumptions of the linear models routinely run on them.
- **Sample-relative CAT scoring** (§3.3) means scores are ordinal-within-batch, not interval-across-batches. Averaging them across studies is not defined.
- **Averaging judges** discards the rater-severity, rater-discrimination, and rater-drift structure that Barth & Stadtmann showed is real. A mean over judges assumes exchangeable, equally-discriminating raters — false by measurement.

**The modern response, and it is genuinely good:**

- **Judge Response Theory** — Myszkowski & Storme (2019), ["Judge Response Theory? A Call to Upgrade Our Psychometrical Account of Creativity Judgments"](https://activityinsight.pace.edu/nmyszkowski/intellcont/Myszkowski%20and%20Storme%20-%202019%20-%20Judge%20response%20theory%20A%20call%20to%20upgrade%20our%20psych-1.pdf). Treat judges as items in an IRT model: each judge gets a difficulty/severity parameter *and* a discrimination parameter, so an uninformative judge is downweighted rather than averaged in. Implemented in the **`jrt` R package**.
- **Many-Facet Rasch Modeling** — Primi, Silvia, Jauk & Benedek (2019), *PACA* 13:176–186, applied MFRM to metaphors, alternate uses, and creative instances, quantifying and adjusting rater severity across multiple tasks. Hung et al. (2012) used MFRM to detect leniency/severity, central tendency, halo, and randomness, finding significant rater × criterion interaction. Uto's Bayesian MFRM adds a Markov component for **rater severity drift**, which is exactly the phenomenon Barth & Stadtmann documented.
- **Count models for DT** — Rasch's Poisson model and two-dimensional extensions for response counts.

The important honest caveat: [Controlling Rater Effects in Divergent Thinking Assessment](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence13060069) (*J. Intelligence*, 2025) found IRT-adjusted and unadjusted scores were **highly correlated** when inter-rater agreement was already high. So JRT/MFRM is insurance against bad panels, not a free accuracy gain on good ones. Its real value is *diagnostic* — it tells you which judges are broken.

**Verdict: the statistical critiques are the most repairable class.** JRT/MFRM largely answer them. They do not touch the construct-validity problems in §1–§4.

## 8. Machine creativity: the specific critiques

### 8.1 Contamination

The tests are public and the manuals are in the training corpus. Stevenson et al. (2022) documented **GPT-3 reproducing content directly from psychology journals and test manuals**. Guzik et al. defended against this by noting the TTCT is proprietary — a weak defense, since TTCT items, sample responses, and scoring exemplars are extensively discussed in the open literature.

### 8.2 Category error / construct transfer

The strongest version, and I think it is correct: TTCT fluency and elaboration are meaningful for humans because generating more ideas is *costly* and reveals something about the generator. For an LLM, **idea count and output length are free sampling parameters.** Fluency is not a measurement of the model, it is a measurement of the decoding configuration. Any TTCT score for an LLM is partly a report of `max_tokens` and temperature.

The nearest thing to a formal statement is Runco's response to Guzik et al. in *JOCB*: tests are administered to examinees rather than initiated by them, which is a problem for a construct tied to intrinsic motivation and spontaneity — DT tests estimate potential for creative problem-solving, not authentic creativity. **A crisp published "category error" argument in exactly those words: UNANSWERED.** It exists mainly as commentary.

### 8.3 The headline studies and what's wrong with them

**Guzik, Byrge & Gilde (2023)**, ["The originality of machines: AI takes the Torrance Test"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2713374523000249), *Journal of Creativity* 33:100065 — the "top 1%" result that produced global headlines. Its actual design:

- **8** ChatGPT test administrations
- **24** University of Montana students as local control
- Norm reference: **2,718 college students tested in 2016**
- Blind-scored by Scholastic Testing Service (a genuine methodological strength)

So "top 1% of human creativity" means: eight AI protocols scored above the 99th percentile of a **2016 college-student** norm sample. It is a percentile against a convenience norm group, with n = 8, on an instrument whose two halves correlate r = .06, using a fluency metric that an LLM controls by hyperparameter.

**Verdict: the claim does not support the headline.** The study is not fraudulent — the blind scoring is real and the finding that LLMs score high on DT tests is robust and has replicated (Hubert et al. 2024, *Scientific Reports*, 151 humans vs GPT-4, higher originality and elaboration even controlling for fluency). What collapses is the inference from "scores high on a DT test" to "is more creative than 99% of people." That inference requires DT validity that §1 shows does not exist.

### 8.4 The rebuttal that used artifacts instead

Chakrabarty, Padmakumar, Brahman & Muresan, ["Art or Artifice? Large Language Models and the False Promise of Creativity"](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2). They built the **Torrance Test of Creative Writing (TTCW)**: 14 binary expert-judged tests over creativity dimensions, applied to whole stories rather than idea lists.

Design: **48 stories** (12 *New Yorker*, 12 each GPT-3.5 / GPT-4 / Claude v1.3), ~1,400 words each; **8 experts** in the formative design phase, **10 different experts** administering evaluation, 3 evaluations per story, **2,016 binary labels**, paid $80 per session.

| Source | Mean TTCW tests passed |
|---|---|
| *New Yorker* | **84.7%** (~11.9 / 14) |
| Claude v1.3 | 30.0% |
| GPT-4 | 27.9% |
| GPT-3.5 | 8.7% |

Professional stories pass **3–10× more** tests. *New Yorker* stories were ranked most-preferred 89% of the time. And critically for anyone building an automated grader: **none of the tested LLMs positively correlated with expert assessments when asked to administer the TTCW themselves — correlations near zero.**

Their own reliability numbers are the honest part: per-test **Fleiss κ = 0.41** (range 0.27–0.66), which is only *moderate* agreement even among paid creative-writing experts. Aggregated to tests-passed-per-story, expert **Pearson r = 0.69**. So even the good instrument is noisy at the item level and only becomes usable through aggregation.

Stated limitations worth respecting: temperature/diversity effects unexplored, prompt engineering not exhausted, no amateur-written control condition, generalization beyond short fiction untested.

### 8.5 LLM-as-judge for creativity specifically

The optimistic baseline everyone cites — GPT-4 achieving >80% agreement with human preferences (Zheng et al., MT-Bench, NeurIPS 2023) — **does not transfer to creativity.**

- Creative writing: **~58% agreement**; open-ended reasoning ~47%.
- Human raters themselves show near-zero agreement on novelty in one hackathon study: **Cohen's κ dropped from 0.18 to 0.04** as rating granularity increased, while usefulness ratings stayed more consistent. The gold standard is unstable in the novelty dimension specifically.
- Expert-domain LLM judging: SME agreement 68% (dietetics), 64% (mental health).
- Judge–expert correlations across a healthcare scoping review ranged **0.40–0.94, median 0.69**, with the low end concentrated in open-ended multi-dimensional tasks — i.e., exactly creativity's shape.
- **Self-preference bias**: LLM judges favor their own generations (Panickssery et al. 2024). In one clinical study self-preference was severe enough to drive **negative** correlation with expert ratings (Kendall's τ = **−0.200**), and an LLM-jury ensemble only reached τ = −0.067.
- Judges are internally inconsistent: the same judge produces different results under pointwise vs. pairwise scoring, and can violate transitivity.
- The circularity critique: LLM judges validated against human labels inherit whatever non-attitudes those humans had. High agreement with an arbitrary human standard is not validity.

**Verdict: WOUNDS severely for absolute creativity scoring; QUALIFIES for within-batch ranking.** An LLM judge scoring artifacts blind on a creativity rubric is, on the current evidence, a noisy instrument whose noise is *correlated with the model's own generation preferences*. That correlation is the dangerous part — it is not mean-zero error, it is a bias toward the grader's own distribution.

### 8.6 Automated originality metrics inherit the confound

Semantic-distance scoring (LSA, SemDis, word2vec/GloVe) does not escape the elaboration problem:

- Forthmann et al. (2019a): LSA-derived semantic distance is **biased by elaboration** — more words yields *lower* LSA semantic distance, so the bias direction can invert relative to human raters.
- Beaty & Johnson (2021), Dumas et al. (2021): **both human raters and semantic-distance models give higher originality to longer responses.**
- Replicated cross-linguistically: in Chinese, human-rated originality correlated with character count at **r = .19**, and TransDis semantic-distance models showed the same length dependence.
- Whether LLM-based scorers (OCSAI) inherit the elaboration bias more or less than human experts is actively being tested and, to my knowledge, **UNANSWERED**.

Static embeddings additionally suffer context insensitivity. Nobody should treat cosine distance as an originality measurement without a length control.

## 9. What a statistician would trust

Ordered by how much validity evidence actually exists, per use case.

**Comparing artifacts within a fixed batch (your case).**
The CAT is the only defensible tradition, and only in this exact configuration:
1. **Rank within batch, never score across batches.** Sample-relative scoring is a feature of the method, not a bug you can wish away. Report ranks or within-batch z-scores; never a portable "creativity = 6.8."
2. **Model judges, don't average them.** Use JRT (Myszkowski's `jrt`) or MFRM (Primi et al. 2019). You get judge severity and discrimination parameters, which lets you *detect* a broken grader instead of averaging it in. This is the single highest-value import from the psychometrics literature into an automated system.
3. **Assume κ ≈ 0.4 at the item level.** Chakrabarty et al. got Fleiss κ = 0.41 with paid experts on 14 well-designed binary tests. Any per-item agreement you observe above that from LLM judges should be treated as suspicious (shared bias) rather than as good news. Aggregate to story level, where the same study got r = 0.69.
4. **Binary expert-style tests beat Likert creativity ratings.** The TTCW design — many specific binary judgments, aggregated — is more defensible than a 1–7 "how creative is this," because it decomposes the construct and makes disagreement diagnosable.
5. **Use heterogeneous judges and check for the cultural appropriateness bias.** The finding that assessors prefer same-culture creators *on appropriateness but not originality* means a monocultural grader pool will systematically penalize out-of-distribution artifacts.

**Never trust, for artifact-level evaluation:**
- Divergent-thinking scores of any kind. r ≈ .18 against achievement, no cross-domain generality, fluency-confounded, and d ≈ .8 reactive to instruction wording.
- Any unpaired novelty metric (semantic distance, DAT-style pairwise distance, distinct-n, uniqueness rates). Proven degenerate optimum: random noise wins. If you use novelty at all, it must be **gated** by an independently-scored appropriateness/quality judgment, with the composite being a conjunction, not a sum. A sum can be maximized by pushing one term.
- Percentile claims against human norm samples. The Guzik pattern — n = 8 against a 2016 college convenience norm — is what those claims usually turn out to be.
- LLM self-evaluation, or any evaluation where the judge model family overlaps the generator family. Self-preference bias is documented and in at least one study drove the judge–expert correlation *negative*.

**Trust conditionally:**
- **CAQ-style achievement criteria**, only with count/zero-inflated models (ZIP, hurdle, negative binomial), never OLS. And read Benedek (2024) first: if potential predictors combine multiplicatively, additive models are misspecified and the field's whole potential→achievement correlation table is an artifact of that misspecification.
- **Human expert panels**, with ≥5 and ≤10 judges (Cseh & Jeffries: outside that band your reliability estimate itself is biased), rating within batch, with judge-level modeling, and with the understanding that individual judges drift over weeks even when panel means don't.

**The three things I would put on a wall for an automated-grading system:**

1. **Reliability is not validity, and this field systematically confuses the two.** A grader with κ = 0.9 that has learned "long and polished = creative" is more dangerous than a noisy one, because its bias is invisible in every reliability statistic you will compute.
2. **Any novelty metric you can optimize, will be optimized, and the optimum is noise.** This is not speculative — it is measured (DAT is beaten by random words; highest-diversity model ranks last on utility).
3. **Creativity scores are not portable.** Sample-relative by construction (CAT), norm-relative by construction (TTCT originality), culture-relative in the appropriateness dimension, and drifting over decades (artists lose ~1 SD of reputation per century post-mortem; PNAS 2023, 3,300+ artists, 1795–2020, steepest decline for those who were most celebrated in life). Build a system that produces comparisons, and refuse to produce absolute numbers. Absolute creativity scores are the field's central fiction.
