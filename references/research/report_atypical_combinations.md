## 1. Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013), *Science* 342(6157):468–472 ✓

Full main text extracted and read. DOI 10.1126/science.1240474.

### 1.1 The method, exactly

**Unit of combination.** Pairwise combinations of references in each paper's bibliography, aggregated *from paper-pairs up to journal-pairs* "to focus on domain-level combinations." Explicitly built on Small (1973) co-citation and Stringer, Sales-Pardo & Amaral (2010, *JASIST* 61:1377).

**Corpus.** 17.9M research articles, Web of Science, publication years 1950–2000. **15,613 journals**, generating "over **122 million** potential journal pairs." ✓

**Null model.** For each publication year, observed co-citation frequency of a journal pair is compared to its frequency in **randomized citation networks**, generated by a **Monte Carlo switching algorithm** over the paper-to-paper citation network. The switching:

> "preserves the total citation counts to and from each paper and the distribution of these citation counts forward and backward in time to ensure that a paper (or journal) with n citations in the observed network will have n citations in the randomized network." ✓

So it is a degree-preserving edge-swap (configuration model) on a *time-directed* citation graph — the temporal constraint is the non-obvious part, and it is what stops the null from producing anachronistic citations.

**⚠️ UNANSWERED: the number of randomized networks.** The main text says "randomized citation networks" (plural) but gives no count. Your brief states 10; I could not verify this — it lives in the supplementary materials (`science.org/content/342/6157/468/suppl/DC1`, Figs. S1–S11, Tables S1–S3), which I could not retrieve. Treat "10" as unconfirmed.

**The z-score.** Per journal pair per year:

```
z_ij,t = (obs_ij,t − mean(rand_ij,t)) / sd(rand_ij,t)
```

⚠️ The paper states only "Comparing the observed frequency with the frequency distribution created with the randomized citation networks, we generated a z score for each journal pair." The explicit formula above is the standard reading, not a quoted equation.

Sign convention (important, and frequently misreported):
- **z > 0** → pair appears *more* often than chance → **conventional**
- **z < 0** → pair appears *less* often than chance → **novel / atypical**

Worked example given ✓: in 1980, *Tetrahedron*–*Experientia* had z = **21.55** (conventional); *Tetrahedron*–*Life Sciences* had z = **−17.67** (atypical).

**Per-paper summary statistics.** Each paper gets a *distribution* of journal-pair z-scores from its reference list. Two order statistics are taken:
1. **Median z** = "median conventionality" — central tendency of the paper's combinations.
2. **10th-percentile z** = "tail novelty" — the left tail, "where novelty may reside."

**The 2×2 split** ✓ — and note the two dimensions are dichotomized *differently*, which is easy to get wrong:
- **Median conventionality**: high/low by **median split** across all papers ("whether the paper's median z score is in the upper or lower half of all median z scores").
- **Tail novelty**: high/low by an **absolute threshold at zero** ("whether the paper's 10th-percentile z score is above or below zero"). **High tail novelty = 10th-pct z BELOW zero.**

**Outcome.** "Hit" = **upper 5th percentile of citations across the whole dataset**, measured by **total citations through 8 years after publication**. ✓

### 1.2 The headline numbers ✓

Sample for the main figure: **all WoS papers 1990–2000**.

| Category | Hit rate per 100 | vs 5% base | Share of WoS papers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High median conventionality + high tail novelty** | **9.11** | **1.82×** | **6.7%** |
| High conventionality + low tail novelty | 5.82 | 1.16× | 23% |
| Low conventionality + high tail novelty | 5.33 | 1.07× | 26% |
| Low conventionality + low tail novelty | 2.05 | 0.41× | 44% |

Differences significant at *P* < 0.00001. The paper describes 9.11 as "nearly twice the background rate" — the precise multiplier vs the 5% base is **1.822×**, and vs the worst quadrant it is **4.44×**. Note the quadrant shares do not map to a clean 2×2 of independent splits (6.7 + 23 + 26 + 44 = 99.7%), because tail novelty is thresholded absolutely, not by median.

**Distributional facts** ✓:
- Median z exceeded **69.0** (1980s) / **99.5** (1990s) for half of all papers — science is overwhelmingly conventional.
- Papers with median z < 0: **3.54%** (1980s), **2.67%** (1990s).
- Papers with 10th-pct z < 0: **40.8%** (1980s), **40.7%** (1990s).

**Teams** ✓: high tail novelty in **36.1% / 39.8% / 49.7%** of papers for solo / dual / 3+ authors. 49.7/36.1 − 1 = **+37.7%**. K-S tests: solo-vs-pair *P* = 0.016, pair-vs-team *P* = 0.001, team-vs-solo *P* < 0.001. Median conventionality shows **no** significant difference by team size (K-S *P* = 0.768 / 0.417 / 0.164). *(Minor internal inconsistency: Fig. 3's caption gives 36.2 / 39.9 / 49.7 while the body text gives 36.1 / 39.8 / 49.7.)*

**Non-monotonicity** ✓: papers binned into 11 equal-size median-conventionality categories, separate regression per bin × team size, field fixed effects. **Peak impact occurs at the 85th–95th percentile of median conventionality, after which the relationship reverses.** This is the most under-cited result in the paper and the one most relevant to transfer: *maximum novelty is not optimal; near-maximum conventionality plus a novel tail is.*

**Robustness reported** ✓: all decades 1950–2000 (Fig. S4); hit defined at top 1% and top 10% (Fig. S5); each of **243 fields** separately (Fig. S6, Table S2); field fixed effects and observable controls. Validation: same-discipline journal pairs have significantly higher z than interdisciplinary pairs (Table S3), though only **40.1%** of interdisciplinary pairs were novel (z<0) in the 1990s — i.e. interdisciplinarity ≠ atypicality.

### 1.3 Failure modes

- **The tail statistic is estimated where the data is thinnest.** 122M possible pairs; a WoS year of ~1M papers × ~30 refs yields ~4×10⁸ pair-observations, i.e. only ~3 per possible pair *on average*, with a viciously skewed distribution. The 10th-percentile z is by construction a *rare-pair* statistic, so it is computed from exactly the pairs whose null variance is worst estimated. Small `sd(rand)` in the denominator makes z explode for rare pairs.
- **Journal is a coarse proxy for concept.** A multidisciplinary journal (*Nature*, *Science*, *PNAS*) pairs atypically with almost everything by construction. This is the core of the Boyack–Klavans critique below.
- **Two dimensions, inconsistent thresholding** (relative median split vs absolute zero) makes the 2×2 non-orthogonal and time-inconsistent.
- **No content.** Two papers citing identical journal sets are indistinguishable regardless of what they say.

### 1.4 Implementation cost

High. Requires the full citation graph (not just reference lists), a temporally-constrained edge-swap MCMC over ~10⁹ edges, replicated, per year. Uzzi et al. note their WoS contract forbade redistribution. Lee, Walsh & Wang (2015) explicitly built a cheaper substitute (§2.1 below).

## 2. Critiques, replications, alternatives

### 2.1 Lee, Walsh & Wang (2015), *Research Policy* 44(3):684–697 ✓

Full text extracted. Adopts Uzzi's two cut-points but **replaces the Monte Carlo null with a closed-form expectation** — "substantially less computationally intensive" ✓:

```
Commonness_ijt = observed_ijt / expected_ijt
              = N_ijt / [ (N_it · N_jt) / N_t ]
              = (N_ijt · N_t) / (N_it · N_jt)
```

where `N_ijt` = count of i–j journal pairs in the year-*t* universe `U_t`, `N_it` = number of pairs containing journal *i*, `N_t` = all pairs. This is an observed/expected ratio under independence — i.e. a **pointwise mutual information ratio**, exactly the analytic limit of a marginal-preserving configuration null. **This is the single most transferable technical result in the whole literature**, because it eliminates the permutation step.

Data: 9,428 WoS papers, publication years 2001–2006, plus survey data on team composition. Findings ✓: team size has an **inverted-U** relation with novelty and a **monotonically increasing** relation with impact; the size–novelty relation is driven by knowledge variety; variety has no direct effect on impact net of novelty and size. Non-response bias check: respondents' papers ~0.1 SD less novel (significant but small).

Note this **partially contradicts Uzzi**: Uzzi finds larger teams monotonically more novel; Lee/Walsh/Wang find an inverted U.

### 2.2 Wang, Veugelers & Stephan (2017), *Research Policy* 46(8):1416–1436 ✓ (NBER WP 22180 text)

A genuinely *different* measure, not a variant of Uzzi's. Novelty is **ex ante** and **binary-at-the-pair-level**:

- A journal pair is **new** if it has **never appeared** in the prior literature (not merely "rarer than chance").
- Each new pair is weighted by **difficulty** = `1 − COS(J1, J2)`, where `COS_{1,2} = (J1·J2)/(‖J1‖‖J2‖)` is the cosine similarity of the two journals' **co-citation profiles** ✓.
- `Novelty_p = Σ over new pairs in p of (1 − COS)`. Papers with no new combinations score 0 ✓.
- Filter: a new combination must be **reused at least once in the next three years** to count (guards against typos/one-offs) ✓.

Categorical variable `NOV_CAT` ✓: (1) non-novel = no new combinations; (2) moderately novel = ≥1 new combination, below the top 1% of its subject category; (3) **highly novel** = top 1% of novelty score within subject category. Median new-combination count: **7** for highly novel vs **1** for moderately novel ✓.

**Results (NBER version, n = 773,311 articles published 2001)** ✓:
- Citations: moderately novel **+2.78%**, highly novel **+12.04%** (GNB).
- **Dispersion +14.90%** for highly novel — the high-risk/high-gain result, and it holds *only* for the highly novel; moderately novel papers are only in the upper tail, not the lower.
- Odds of a **top-1% "big hit"**: **+39.82%** (highly novel), **+11.22%** (moderately novel).
- Odds of being cited by a big hit: **+3.56%** for moderately novel (much smaller than highly novel).
- **Delayed recognition** ✓: highly novel papers are *less* likely to be top-cited with windows **shorter than 3 years**; significantly *more* likely from **year 4** onward, with the advantage growing in window length. Moderately novel papers are disadvantaged below **5 years** and only gain significance at **≥9 years**.
- Novel papers are more cited in **foreign** fields but not home fields; and are published in **lower-than-expected Impact Factor** journals.

⚠️ **Discrepancy flagged.** The published *Research Policy* abstract (and the LSE Impact Blog summary) reports **n = 1,056,936** and "**more than triple** the probability of being a top 1% highly cited paper" with a long citation window. The NBER working-paper text I verified reports n = 773,311 and **+39.82% odds**. These are different specifications/samples. **I could not verify the "triple" figure or the 1,056,936 sample against the published primary source** (ScienceDirect paywall). Use the NBER numbers as the verified ones; treat "triple" as UNANSWERED.

**The policy punchline is the important one for you:** short-window citation metrics and JIF are *actively biased against* the thing you are trying to measure. Any offline novelty metric validated against short-horizon reception is validating against the wrong signal.

### 2.4 Foster, Rzhetsky & Evans (2015), *American Sociological Review* 80(5):875–908 ✓

Extracted (arXiv 1302.6906). This is the **strongest and cleanest** result in the whole science-of-science literature for your purposes, because it does not use journals at all — it uses **content entities**.

**Method** ✓: build a time-ordered network over **chemicals** from MEDLINE annotations in biomedical chemistry, 1983–2008. Each published relationship is classified into one of **five strategies**:
1. **Jump** — a relationship involving completely unexplored entities
2. **New consolidation** — new link *within* an existing knowledge cluster
3. **New bridge** — new link *between* clusters
4. **Repeat consolidation**
5. **Repeat bridge**

Clusters are found by partitioning the chemical network into subfields.

**Prevalence** ✓: repeats **85.8%** vs new-or-jump **14.2%** (≈6×). New bridges + new consolidations **12.4%** vs jumps **1.8%**. Distribution is **remarkably stable 1983–2008** despite the opportunity space exploding — new-link opportunities outnumbered known links by a factor of **22 in 1983, growing to 188 by 2008**. Scientists did *not* follow the opportunity.

**Risk/reward** ✓, using **information-theoretic surprisal** `I(p_i) = −log(p_i)` on the *strategy frequency*:
- A strategy an order of magnitude less probable (0.5 → 0.05) earns **+2.26 citations** on average.
- Surprisal explains **29.2%** of variation in mean citations (*R*² = 0.292); adding year → **76%** combined.
- Surprisal explains **28.6%** of variation in the **standard deviation** of citations (*F*(1,98) = 24.68, *p* < 0.001); combined with year → **44.4%**.
- **Verdict** ✓: "the additional reward is insufficient to compensate for the additional risk." Analysis of winners of **137 prizes** in biomedicine and chemistry shows the "occasional gamble for extraordinary impact" is the most plausible explanation for observed risk-taking levels.

**Why this matters most for transfer:** Foster et al. define novelty over an **entity-relation graph built from content**, not from a citation network, and score novelty by **surprisal of a strategy class** rather than a per-pair z-score. Both properties survive the move to generated artifacts. See §5.

## 3. Patent measures

### 3.1 Originality & Generality — Trajtenberg, Henderson & Jaffe (1997); formulas verified via Hall, Jaffe & Trajtenberg (2001), NBER WP 8498 ✓

Exact, as printed in HJT 2001 §4 and Appendix 2 ✓:

```
Generality_i  = 1 − Σ_{j=1..n_i} s_ij²      s_ij = share of citations RECEIVED by patent i from class j
Originality_i = 1 − Σ_{j=1..n_i} s_ij²      s_ij = share of citations MADE by patent i to class j
```

Both are **1 − Herfindahl** over patent classes (3-digit USPC in the NBER file). Data: ~3M US patents (Jan 1963–Dec 1999), >16M citations 1975–1999 ✓.

**Their own caveat, verbatim in a footnote** ✓: *"Note that these measures depend of course upon the patent classification system: a finer classification would render higher measures, and conversely for a coarser system."* And in text ✓: "there is an inherent element of arbitrariness in them." **The measure is not scale-free — it is a property of the taxonomy as much as of the artifact.** This is the single most important warning for transfer.

**Validation** ✓ is weak and qualitative: Computers & Communications scores highest on generality (fits the General Purpose Technology story), Mechanical/Others lowest, Chemical surprisingly high. HJT call this "a sort of 'validation'." There is **no external ground-truth validation** of originality/generality in HJT 2001.

### 3.2 The small-sample bias and its exact correction (Hall, Appendix 2) ✓

This is the part everyone cites and almost nobody implements. Fully extracted:

Sample Herfindahl `HHI = Σ_j (N_j/N)²` estimating population `η = Σ_j λ_j²`. Under a multinomial with `E[N_j²] = Nλ_j + N(N−1)λ_j²`:

```
E[HHI | N] = 1/N + ((N−1)/N)·η
Bias:  E[HHI|N] − η = (1 − η)/N          ← concentration biased UPWARD
```

Unbiased Herfindahl estimator ✓:
```
η̂ = (N·HHI − 1)/(N − 1)
```

For generality `G_i = 1 − Σ_j (N_ij/N_i)²` estimating `γ_i = 1 − η_i` ✓:
```
E[G_i | N_i] = ((N_i − 1)/N_i)·γ_i
Bias:  E[G_i|N_i] − γ_i = −γ_i / N_i     ← diversity biased DOWNWARD
Unbiased:  γ̂_i = (N_i/(N_i − 1))·G_i
```

Hall also gives the variance correction ✓: `Var(η̂) = (1/M)·Σ_k [N_k²·Var(HHI_k)/(N_k−1)²]`; the naive standard error of the mean Herfindahl is biased downward by `(N−1)/N`.

**Truncation** ✓: HJT are explicit that the steep late-period decline in generality "is almost surely due to truncation" and that "the adjustment described in the previous paragraph **mitigates but does not eliminate** this decline" — residual bias because near-in-technology-space citations arrive *sooner*, so early-window generality is downward-biased even after the count correction. Their two proposed fixes ✓: the **fixed-effects approach** (scale citations by the average for the patent's cohort/class group) and the **quasi-structural approach** (econometrically separate the multiple effects on citation rates).

### 3.3 Other patent novelty measures

- **Fleming (2001), *Management Science* 47:117–132** — novelty as **new combinations of patent subclasses**; the finding is that new combinations produce **higher variance** in usefulness, not higher mean. ⚠️ Verified only via citations in Lee/Walsh/Wang ✓ and Wang/Veugelers/Stephan ✓, both of which describe it as "the occurrence of new combinations of subclasses to which a patent is assigned … a measure of the generative creativity of the inventors." Exact formulas and effect sizes: **UNANSWERED**.
- **Verhoeven, Bakker & Veugelers (2016), *Research Policy* 45:707–723** — combines the combinatorial-novelty measure with **novelty in technological/scientific knowledge origins**, i.e. whether the patent cites technologies or scientific literature from areas **never cited before** by patents in its class. ⚠️ Verified only via the one-sentence description in NBER w22180 ✓. Exact definitions, IPC level, and validation numbers: **UNANSWERED**.
- **Kelly, Papanikolaou, Seru & Taddy (2021), *AER: Insights* 3:303–320** ✓ — fully extracted (NBER w25266). **Text-based, no classes, no citations.** This is the best-designed patent novelty measure and the most transferable.

  Uses TF-IDF with a **retrospective / "point-in-time" IDF** ✓ — critically, standard IDF is computed over the whole sample and therefore *penalizes* a term that became common *later*. Their worked example ✓: Tesla's 1888 AC motor patent (US 381,968) was among the first to use "alternating current"; standard IDF would de-weight it because the 20th century used the phrase constantly, giving "a misleading, and quite inverted, portrayal of the patent's innovativeness." So IDF is computed using only documents **prior to** the focal patent.

  ```
  BS_j  = Σ_{i ∈ B_{j,τ}} ρ_{i,j}     backward similarity — prior patents filed in τ years before j (τ = 5 baseline)
  FS_jτ = Σ_{i ∈ F_{j,τ}} ρ_{j,i}     forward similarity — patents filed in τ years after j
  q_jτ  = FS_jτ / BS_j                 "quality" / significance
  ```
  **Low BS = novel** (deviates from state of the art); **high FS = impactful**. The ratio isolates patents that are *both*. Results insensitive to τ ✓. Time fixed effects removed when building indices, to strip mechanical drift in language and document counts ✓.

  **Validation** ✓ — three checks, and this is the best-validated novelty measure in this entire report: (1) a hand-identified list of historically important 19th/20th-century patents scores high; (2) the measure **predicts future citations** — "within a few years of a patent's arrival, text-based similarity measures reach an assessment of patent quality that predicts citation counts decades henceforth"; (3) it **correlates strongly with market value** (Kogan et al. 2017 stock-reaction measure). Coverage 1840–2010; the indices predict aggregate and sectoral productivity.

### 4.1 Vargas & Castells (2011), RecSys '11, 109–116 ⚠️ UNVERIFIED

The unifying scheme: a novelty/diversity metric is an expectation over the ranking, decomposed into three independent factors —

```
m(R) = C · Σ_{k=1..|R|} disc(k) · p(rel | i_k, u) · nov(i_k)
```

- **rank discount** `disc(k)` — typically `1/log₂(k+1)` (nDCG-style) or `0.85^(k−1)` (RBP-style)
- **relevance / choice** `p(rel|i,u)` — makes the metric relevance-aware, so a diverse list of irrelevant junk does not score
- **novelty model** `nov(i)` — pluggable

The named instantiations:
- **EPC** (Expected Popularity Complement): `nov(i) = 1 − p(seen|i)`
- **EFD** (Expected Free Discovery): `nov(i) = −log₂ p(seen|i)` — self-information
- **EPD** (Expected Profile Distance): `nov(i) = ` mean distance from *i* to the user's profile items
- **EILD** (Expected Intra-List Diversity): `nov(i) = ` mean distance from *i* to the other items *in the same ranking*

**All four are the same formula with a different `nov`.** That factorization is the durable contribution and it does transfer.

Popularity-based self-information novelty, standard form ⚠️:
```
nov(i) = −log₂( |U_i| / |U| )
```
where `U_i` = users who have interacted with item *i*.

### 4.2 Serendipity ⚠️ UNVERIFIED

**Ge, Delgado-Battenfeld & Jannach (2010), RecSys '10** — serendipity requires a **primitive model** `PM` (a trivially-predictable baseline recommender):
```
UNEXP    = RS \ PM
SRDP     = |UNEXP ∩ USEFUL| / |RS|
```
Unexpectedness is *relative to a baseline*, and serendipity = **unexpected AND useful**. Novelty ≠ serendipity: an item can be novel (unseen) but entirely expected.

**Murakami, Mori & Orihara (2008)** ⚠️ use a `max(0, P(s) − P_prim(s))` unexpectedness term against a habitual predictor. **Adamopoulos & Tuzhilin (2014)** ⚠️ define unexpectedness against an explicit *set of expectations*. **Kotkov, Wang & Veijalainen (2016)** ⚠️ survey decomposes serendipity into **relevance + novelty + unexpectedness**. **Zhang et al. (2012) Auralist** ⚠️ uses an "unserendipity" metric. Exact formulas: **UNANSWERED**.

### 4.3 Intra-list similarity — Ziegler, McNee, Konstan & Lausen (2005), WWW '05 ✓

Extracted. ILS is the sum of pairwise similarities over all unordered pairs in the top-*N* list, halved:
```
ILS(P_wi) = [ Σ_{b_k ∈ ℑP_wi} Σ_{b_e ∈ ℑP_wi, b_e ≠ b_k} c°(b_k, b_e) ] / 2
```
**Higher ILS = lower diversity** ✓. And the property they flag explicitly (their Eq. 6) ✓:
```
∀ σ_i, σ_j ∈ S_N :  ILS(P_wi ∘ σ_i) = ILS(P_wi ∘ σ_j)
```
**ILS is permutation-insensitive** — reordering the list does not change it. This is exactly the gap Vargas & Castells's rank discount later filled.

**The user study** ✓ — and this is real, rare evidence:
- Offline: 361,349 ratings. Online: BookCrossing, mailed to eligible users with unique encrypted per-user links; **2,125 users completed** the study over a 3-week window. 5-point Likert, one-factor ANOVA.
- Three questions: per-item accuracy, **perceived "covered range"** (broad vs narrow range of reading interests), and **overall list satisfaction**.
- Result ✓: **topic diversification negatively correlates with pure accuracy** — higher diversification factor Θ_F monotonically lowers single-vote average scores (item-based CF *p* ≪ 0.01; user-based *p* < 0.1). Users perceived user-based CF as significantly more accurate than item-based at every Θ_F.
- The paper's abstract claim ✓: diversification "improves user satisfaction with recommendation lists, **in particular for lists generated using the common item-based collaborative filtering algorithm**" — i.e. the satisfaction gain was **algorithm-conditional**, not universal, and came *at a measured cost in accuracy*.

That conditional is the honest headline: **the accuracy/diversity tradeoff is real and measured; the satisfaction payoff is contingent.**

The one hard user-study number I *did* verify (Ziegler, n=2,125 ✓) shows diversification **reducing** measured accuracy while improving satisfaction only for one algorithm family. Take the field's offline novelty metrics as **construct-plausible and empirically under-validated**. If you build on them, you own the validation.

## 5. TRANSFER VERDICT

Setting: a **batch of generated artifacts** (text, ideas, designs, code). **No citation network. No large corpus of prior combinations.** Batch size realistically 10–500.

### 5.1 Uzzi-style atypical-combination z-score — **DOES NOT TRANSFER within-batch.** Transfers only with an external reference corpus.

**Minimum data requirement, derived.** The z-score is **not a property of the focal artifact**. It is a property of a *pair of concept tags*, estimated across a corpus; the artifact contributes only a lookup and two order statistics. So the requirement is on the corpus:

Let `V` = concept-tag vocabulary, `K` = tags per artifact, `N` = corpus size.
- Possible pairs: `V(V−1)/2`
- Pair-observations contributed: `N · K(K−1)/2`
- For a mean of `c` observations per possible pair: **`N ≥ c · V(V−1) / [K(K−1)]`**

Worked: `V = 200`, `K = 8`, target `c = 10` → `N ≈ 10 · 39,800/56 ≈ **7,100 artifacts**`. At `V = 1,000`, `K = 8` → **≈178,000**. And `c = 10` is *generous*; the tail statistic you actually care about (10th percentile) is estimated on the pairs with the fewest observations, so effective requirements are worse than the mean suggests. Uzzi himself is only marginally above this bar: 122M possible pairs against ~4×10⁸ pair-observations per year ≈ 3 per pair on average.

**A batch of 20–100 generated artifacts is off by two to three orders of magnitude.** Computing z-scores from the batch itself is not a small-sample problem, it is a category error: every pair would be observed 0 or 1 times, `sd(rand)` would be ~0 for most pairs, and z would be pure noise amplified by a near-zero denominator.

**What *does* work — and I'd build this:**

1. **Fix the reference table externally.** Compute `Commonness_ij` on a corpus of **10⁴–10⁵ domain documents** (papers, prior designs, an issue tracker, a code corpus, a product-idea archive), tagged by the *same* tagger you apply to generated artifacts. Then generated artifacts are scored by lookup only, and batch size becomes irrelevant.
2. **Skip the permutation null entirely — use Lee, Walsh & Wang's closed form** ✓:
   ```
   Commonness_ij = (N_ij · N_total) / (N_i · N_j)
   ```
   This is the analytic expectation under a marginal-preserving (configuration) null and is what a permutation test converges to. Monte Carlo buys you a variance estimate for a z-score; if you don't need the z's calibration, you don't need the permutations. **This alone removes ~90% of the implementation cost.** Score with `log(Commonness)` — it is a PMI, well-behaved and symmetric around 0.
3. **If you do want a z**, the permutation null is a **bipartite configuration model**: shuffle tags across artifacts preserving (a) each artifact's tag count and (b) each tag's total frequency. Curveball / degree-preserving swap. ~100 replicates suffice for a stable `sd` when counts are non-trivial; below ~5 observed occurrences it will not stabilize at any replicate count.
4. **Keep both statistics, and keep the shape.** Uzzi's actual finding is *not* "novel is good." It is: **high conventionality (85th–95th percentile — near-maximum, then it reverses) plus a novel left tail**. A batch evaluator that reports only a novelty score is reproducing the failure mode Uzzi's paper exists to refute. Report median-PMI and 10th-percentile-PMI as a **pair**, and treat the target region as high-median / low-tail.

**Failure modes specific to the generated-artifact setting — take these seriously:**
- **Tagger-induced co-occurrence.** If tags come from an LLM or embedding clustering, tag co-occurrence encodes the *tagger's* priors. "Atypical" may then measure tagger idiosyncrasy, not artifact novelty. If the generator and the tagger share a base model, the errors are correlated and the metric is partly self-referential. This is the analogue of Boyack–Klavans's disciplinary confound and is likely worse.
- **Taxonomy dependence** (HJT's warning ✓ transfers verbatim): a finer tag vocabulary mechanically raises novelty. Tag count `K` must be held constant across artifacts or the comparison is invalid.
- **Rare-tag instability.** Any tag appearing <5 times in the reference corpus should be pooled or dropped.

**Implementation cost with the external-corpus + closed-form design:** low. One tagging pass over the reference corpus, one sparse co-occurrence matrix, one lookup per artifact-pair. Days, not weeks. Cost with a faithful Uzzi replication: weeks, plus a citation graph you don't have.

### 5.2 Foster/Rzhetsky/Evans surprisal over a strategy taxonomy — **BEST TRANSFER in the science-of-science group.** ✓

Foster et al. never touch a citation network — they build an **entity-relation graph from content** and score the **surprisal of a strategy class**, `I(p) = −log p`. Both properties survive.

For generated artifacts: extract concept entities, build the co-occurrence graph over the *reference corpus*, cluster it, then classify each artifact's concept-links into **jump / new-bridge / new-consolidation / repeat-bridge / repeat-consolidation** and score `−log(strategy frequency)`.

Why it beats the Uzzi transfer:
- The statistic is over **five classes**, not 10⁴–10⁸ pairs. Frequencies are estimable from a batch of a few hundred artifacts. **The data requirement collapses by orders of magnitude.**
- It is a *categorical* judgment ("is this link new? is it within or across clusters?") — robust to the exact similarity values, which is where embedding-based methods are shakiest.
- It gives you the risk/variance story for free (surprisal explained **28.6%** of citation *SD* ✓), which is what you want when evaluating a *batch*: batch-level dispersion is a first-class output, not a nuisance.

**Minimum data:** a reference corpus large enough to cluster the entity graph (~10³–10⁴ documents), plus the batch. **Cost: moderate.** The honest caveat: cluster boundaries are a free parameter, and the "jump" class will be rare and noisy at small batch size.

### 5.3 Herfindahl originality (Trajtenberg/Jaffe/Henderson) — **TRANSFERS, with one severe caveat.** ✓

**This is the only measure here that needs no corpus at all.** `Originality = 1 − Σ_j s_j²` over the focal artifact's *own* distribution of component classes. Computable on a single artifact in isolation. That is a genuine advantage over everything else in this report.

**Generality does not transfer.** It requires forward citations. There is no forward-citation analogue for a generated artifact. Dead on arrival.

**The severe caveat — small-*N* bias, quantified.** HJT's exact result ✓: `E[G|N] = ((N−1)/N)·γ`, bias `= −γ/N`.

| `N` (components/tags) | Downward bias as % of true γ |
|---|---|
| 5 | **−20%** |
| 10 | −10% |
| 30 | −3.3% |
| 100 | −1.0% |

Artifacts typically carry 5–15 concept tags. **At `N` = 5–10 the measure is dominated by how many tags the tagger emitted, not by the artifact's actual diversity.** Two artifacts with identical true diversity but 5 vs 15 tags will differ by ~13% of γ purely from the bias term.

Worse — the correction **breaks down** exactly where you need it. `γ̂ = (N/(N−1))·G`. Take `N = 5` tags, all distinct: `HHI = 5·(1/5)² = 0.2`, `G = 0.8`, and `γ̂ = (5/4)·0.8 = **1.0**` — implying zero population concentration, which is impossible for any finite class set. **The estimator saturates and becomes degenerate below roughly `N` = 10–15.**

**Verdict:** usable *only* if you hold `N` fixed across the batch by construction (force exactly `K` tags per artifact), apply the `N/(N−1)` correction, and never compare across different `K`. Under those constraints it is cheap and defensible. **Cost: trivial** (a few lines). **Do not ship it with variable tag counts.**

### 5.4 Kelly et al. backward/forward similarity — **the backward half transfers well.** ✓

`BS_j = Σ_{i ∈ prior} ρ_{i,j}` — **low backward similarity = novel**. This needs only (a) a prior corpus and (b) a similarity function. Embeddings supply the latter directly. **This is the most straightforwardly implementable rigorous novelty measure in the report**, and it is the best-validated one (predicts citations decades out, correlates with market value ✓).

Two transferable design lessons from their construction ✓:
- **Point-in-time IDF.** Their central technical move — never let a term's later ubiquity retroactively de-weight an early use of it. For batch evaluation the analogue is: **compute the reference statistics on a corpus strictly prior to the generation, and freeze them.** If you recompute the reference corpus to include the generated batch, novel-but-repeated ideas within the batch will suppress each other's scores. Freeze the reference.
- **Time fixed effects removed** before building indices ✓ — the analogue is removing prompt/topic fixed effects before comparing artifacts across prompts.

`FS` (forward similarity) requires the future. **Does not transfer** — *unless* you deliberately construct a "future": generate a second wave of artifacts conditioned on the first, and measure how much wave-2 resembles each wave-1 artifact. That is a real, buildable proxy for influence and I'd flag it as the most interesting untried idea in this report. **Marked as my inference, not from any source.**

### 5.5 Recommender metrics

| Family | Transfers? | Minimum data | Cost | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Intra-list diversity / ILS / EILD** | **YES — fully** | The batch alone + an embedding | Trivial | Only metric here needing *nothing* external |
| **Popularity novelty (`−log p`)** | Partially | A popularity/frequency proxy | Low | Proxy choice is the whole ballgame |
| **Unexpectedness vs primitive model** | **YES — strongest conceptual fit** | A baseline generator | Low | Underrated; build this |
| **Serendipity = unexpected ∩ relevant** | Yes, but | A relevance judge | Medium–high | Cost is the judge, not the metric |

**ILS/EILD — the honest case.** Computable purely within-batch from pairwise embedding distances; no corpus, no popularity, no citations. Use the Vargas & Castells rank-discounted form if the batch is ranked, because plain ILS is **provably permutation-insensitive** (Ziegler Eq. 6 ✓) and therefore blind to whether your good items are first.

Failure modes, and they are not minor:
- **Embedding anisotropy.** Raw LLM embeddings occupy a narrow cone; cosine distances compress into a small range, so ILS differences are dominated by scale artifacts. Whiten/center before use, and report the distance distribution, not just its mean.
- **It rewards outliers and gibberish.** An incoherent artifact maximizes mean pairwise distance. **ILS alone is trivially gameable and must be paired with a quality gate** — this is precisely why Vargas & Castells multiply by `p(rel|i,u)`. Do not ship un-relevance-weighted diversity.
- **Batch-size sensitivity.** Mean pairwise distance drifts with `N`; never compare ILS across batches of different size without normalization.
- **It measures spread, not meaningfulness.** Ten artifacts spread across ten irrelevant directions beat ten artifacts exploring one hard problem from ten angles.

**Popularity novelty.** No interaction matrix exists. Two defensible proxies: (a) **reference-corpus n-gram / concept frequency** — closest to the original construct and I'd prefer it; (b) **sequence log-probability under a reference LM**, i.e. `−log p(artifact)`. Proxy (b) is seductive and dangerous: it conflates *novel* with *improbable*, and the most improbable text is degenerate text. If you use (b), condition on fluency.

**Unexpectedness vs a primitive model — the best recommender-side transfer, and it is under-appreciated.** Ge et al.'s `PM` maps *exactly* onto a cheap baseline generator: temperature-0 / greedy decode, or a bare unelaborated prompt. Then `UNEXP = batch \ PM_output`, operationalized as distance from the baseline's output distribution. This is **more appropriate to generated artifacts than to recommenders**, because in recommenders `PM` is a hand-waved "trivially predictable" model, whereas here you can *actually run it*. Minimum data: one extra baseline generation pass. Cost: low. This is the highest value-per-effort item in the report.

**Serendipity.** `SRDP = |UNEXP ∩ USEFUL| / |RS|` requires a relevance judgment. For generated artifacts nobody supplies it for free — it's an LLM judge or a human. The metric is cheap; **the judge is the entire cost, and the judge is the thing that needs validating.** Note the reliability warning from §4.4: this literature has essentially not validated its offline metrics against perceived novelty, so do not inherit its confidence.

### 5.6 What I would actually build

Ranked by value per unit effort:

1. **Unexpectedness vs an actually-executed primitive baseline** (Ge et al. ✓ conceptually; trivially operationalized here). One extra generation pass.
2. **Backward similarity to a frozen prior corpus** (Kelly et al. ✓) — low `BS` = novel. Freeze the reference corpus before generation. Best-validated construct in the report.
3. **Relevance-weighted, rank-discounted intra-list diversity** (Vargas & Castells form ⚠️, ILS ✓) — never unweighted.
4. **Median + 10th-percentile PMI over concept-tag pairs against an external reference corpus**, using Lee/Walsh/Wang's closed form ✓ instead of Monte Carlo. Requires ~10⁴ reference documents. **Report both statistics and target high-median/novel-tail — not maximum novelty.**
5. **Foster-style strategy surprisal** ✓ if you can cluster the concept graph — cheapest path to a *risk/variance* story about the batch.
6. Herfindahl originality ✓ only with fixed `K` and the `N/(N−1)` correction. Skip below `K` = 15.

**And the thing to not do:** compute Uzzi z-scores within a batch of generated artifacts. The pair-observation counts are 0 or 1, the null's standard deviation is ~0, and the resulting z-scores are noise divided by noise. The requirement is `N ≳ c·V²/K²` reference documents, and no batch is going to satisfy it.
